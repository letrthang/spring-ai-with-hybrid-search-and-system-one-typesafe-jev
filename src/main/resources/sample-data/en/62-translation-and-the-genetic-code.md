# Biotech basics — translation and the genetic code

## What translation is
Translation is the process in which a ribosome reads a messenger RNA and builds the matching protein. The mRNA is
read in groups of three bases called codons, and each codon is matched to one amino acid carried by a transfer RNA.
Translation is the second step of gene expression, after transcription, and happens in the cytoplasm of all cells.

## Codons and the genetic code
The genetic code is the set of rules that links each three-base codon to an amino acid. With four bases there are 4
× 4 × 4 = 64 possible codons: 61 code for amino acids and 3 are stop signals. For example, GCU codes for alanine
and UGG codes for tryptophan. The genetic code was worked out in the 1960s, starting with the discovery in 1961
that UUU codes for phenylalanine.

## A redundant but unambiguous code
The genetic code is redundant, which means most amino acids are coded by more than one codon. Leucine, serine and
arginine each have six codons, while methionine and tryptophan have only one each. The genetic code is also
unambiguous, because each codon means just one amino acid, and the third base of a codon can often change without
changing the amino acid, which makes many mutations silent.

## Start and stop codons
The start codon AUG tells the ribosome where to begin translation and also codes for the amino acid methionine. The
three stop codons, UAA, UAG and UGA, code for no amino acid; instead, proteins called release factors recognise
them and free the finished protein. A mutation that creates an early stop codon, called a nonsense mutation,
therefore usually gives a short protein that does not work.

## The reading frame
The reading frame is the way an mRNA sequence is divided into consecutive codons, starting from the start codon.
Any sequence can be read in three frames, depending on whether reading begins at the first, second or third base.
Inserting or deleting one or two bases shifts the reading frame, which is called a frameshift mutation, and changes
every codon after that point.

## Transfer RNA and the anticodon
Transfer RNA (tRNA) is the adapter molecule that brings the correct amino acid to the ribosome during translation.
Each tRNA is about 76–90 nucleotides long, folds into an L shape and carries an anticodon, three bases that pair
with a codon on the mRNA. Enzymes called aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases attach the right amino acid to each tRNA, with
one synthetase for each of the 20 amino acids.

## The ribosome
The ribosome is the molecular machine that reads mRNA and joins amino acids into a protein. A ribosome has a small
subunit that holds the mRNA and a large subunit that forms peptide bonds; in bacteria these are the 30S and 50S
subunits of a 70S ribosome, and in eukaryotes the 40S and 60S subunits of an 80S ribosome. The peptide bonds are
actually made by ribosomal RNA, so the ribosome is a ribozyme.

## Initiation, elongation and termination
Translation runs in three stages: initiation, elongation and termination. In initiation, the small subunit finds
the start codon, the first tRNA carrying methionine binds, and the large subunit joins. In elongation, tRNAs pass
through the A, P and E sites in turn while the chain grows by about 15–20 amino acids per second in bacteria and
about 5 in eukaryotes, and termination occurs when a stop codon reaches the A site.

## Polysomes and protein folding
A polysome is a single mRNA being translated by several ribosomes at the same time, spaced along the message like
beads on a string. Polysomes let a cell make many copies of a protein quickly from one mRNA. As the new chain
leaves the ribosome it begins to fold, often with help from chaperone proteins, and many proteins are then
modified, for example by adding phosphate or sugar groups.

## Antibiotics that target translation
Many antibiotics work by blocking the bacterial ribosome, which differs from the human one. Tetracycline stops
tRNAs from entering the A site of the 30S subunit, streptomycin makes the ribosome misread the code, and
chloramphenicol and erythromycin act on the 50S subunit. Because human cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S, these
antibiotics can stop bacterial protein synthesis with limited harm to human cells.
