# Biotech basics — microbiology

## What microbes are
Microbes are living things too small to see with the naked eye, including bacteria, archaea, fungi such as yeast,
algae and protozoa. Viruses are usually studied alongside them, although they are not cells. Most microbes are
harmless or helpful, and only a small fraction cause disease.

## Bacterial shapes
Bacteria come in three basic shapes. Round bacteria are called cocci, rod-shaped bacteria are called bacilli and
spiral bacteria are called spirilla or spirochetes. Shape, together with how cells group into pairs, chains or
clusters, is one of the first clues used to identify an unknown bacterium under the microscope.

## The Gram stain
The Gram stain sorts bacteria into two groups by their cell wall. Gram-positive bacteria have a thick peptidoglycan
wall that keeps the crystal violet dye and look purple. Gram-negative bacteria have a thin wall plus an outer
membrane, lose the purple dye and take up the pink counterstain, safranin.

## Bacterial growth curve
A bacterial culture grows in four phases. In the lag phase cells adapt to the new medium; in the log phase they
divide at a steady rate; in the stationary phase nutrients run low and growth stops. In the death phase more cells die
than are made.

## Doubling time
Doubling time is the time a bacterial population takes to double in number during the log phase. Escherichia coli
can double about every 20 minutes in rich medium at 37 °C, so one cell could become more than a million in under 7
hours. Slow growers such as the tuberculosis bacterium need about 15–20 hours.

## Culture media and agar plates
A culture medium is a liquid or gel that supplies the nutrients microbes need to grow. Liquid medium, called broth,
is used to grow large numbers of cells, while agar plates, made by adding about 1.5% agar, give a solid surface.
Selective media contain substances that let only certain microbes grow.

## Colonies and CFU
A colony is a visible spot on an agar plate that grew from a single cell or a small clump of cells. Counting colonies
gives the number of colony-forming units (CFU) in the sample. Plates with 30 to 300 colonies are counted, and the
result is multiplied by the dilution factor.

## Aerobic and anaerobic microbes
Microbes differ in how they use oxygen. Obligate aerobes need oxygen to grow, obligate anaerobes are harmed by it,
and facultative anaerobes such as E. coli and yeast grow with or without it. Anaerobes are grown in sealed jars or
chambers from which the oxygen has been removed.

## Bacterial spores
Some bacteria, such as Bacillus and Clostridium, form endospores when conditions become harsh. An endospore is a
tough, dormant form that survives boiling, drying and many disinfectants for years. Killing spores needs stronger
treatment, such as an autoclave at 121 °C for at least 15 minutes.

## Antibiotics and resistance
An antibiotic is a drug that kills bacteria or stops them from growing, for example by blocking cell wall building or
protein synthesis. Antibiotics do not work against viruses. Resistance appears when bacteria gain genes or mutations
that let them survive the drug, and overuse of antibiotics speeds this up.
