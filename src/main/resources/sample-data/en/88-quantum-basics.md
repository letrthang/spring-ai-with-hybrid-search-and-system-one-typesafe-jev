# Physics basics — quantum ideas

## What quantum physics is
Quantum physics is the physics of very small things, such as atoms, electrons and photons, where energy comes in
separate packets called quanta. The idea began in 1900, when Max Planck explained the light given off by hot objects
by assuming that energy is emitted in steps of E = h f. Quantum physics explains why atoms are stable, how lasers and
transistors work, and why chemistry behaves as it does.

## The photoelectric effect
The photoelectric effect is the release of electrons from a metal surface when light shines on it. Electrons escape
only if each photon has more energy than the metal's work function, so dim ultraviolet light frees electrons from
zinc, with a work function of about 4.3 eV, while bright red light frees none. Albert Einstein explained this in 1905
with Ek max = h f − φ and won the 1921 Nobel Prize for it.

## Energy levels in atoms
Electrons in an atom can only have certain fixed energies, called energy levels, rather than any energy at all. In
hydrogen the levels are En = −13.6 eV / n², so the ground state, n = 1, is −13.6 eV and the first excited state, n =
2, is −3.4 eV. An electron moves between levels only by absorbing or emitting exactly the energy difference, often as
a single photon.

## Line spectra
A line spectrum is the set of sharp colours that a gas emits when its electrons drop from higher to lower energy
levels. Each drop gives a photon with energy equal to the gap between the levels, so hydrogen emits red light at 656
nm when an electron falls from n = 3 to n = 2, a drop of 1.89 eV. Every element has its own pattern of lines, which
lets astronomers identify the elements in distant stars.

## Wave–particle duality
Wave–particle duality means that light and matter show both wave behaviour and particle behaviour. Light spreads and
interferes like a wave in the double-slit experiment, yet it is detected as single photons arriving at single points
on a screen. Even when photons or electrons are sent through the slits one at a time, the dots slowly build up the
same striped interference pattern.

## Matter waves and the de Broglie wavelength
Every moving particle has a de Broglie wavelength, λ = h / p, where p is its momentum and h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. An
electron accelerated through 100 V has a wavelength of about 0.12 nm, similar to the spacing of atoms in a crystal, so
electrons diffract off crystals. A 60 g tennis ball at 50 m/s has a wavelength of about 2 × 10⁻³⁴ m, far too small
ever to notice.

## The uncertainty principle
The Heisenberg uncertainty principle states that a particle's position and momentum cannot both be known exactly at
the same time. In symbols, Δx × Δp ≥ h / 4π, so pinning down an electron's position more closely makes its momentum
more uncertain. The uncertainty principle is a basic property of nature, not a fault of measuring instruments, and it
explains why electrons do not fall into the nucleus.

## The wavefunction and probability
In quantum physics a particle is described by a wavefunction, and the square of the wavefunction gives the probability
of finding the particle at each place. Erwin Schrödinger wrote the equation for the wavefunction in 1926. Quantum
physics predicts only the probabilities of measurement results, so the electron in a hydrogen atom is pictured as a
cloud showing where it is likely to be found rather than as a tiny planet on an orbit.

## Quantum tunnelling
Quantum tunnelling is the ability of a particle to pass through an energy barrier that it does not have enough energy
to climb over. Tunnelling becomes likely only when the barrier is extremely thin, typically about a nanometre for
electrons. Tunnelling explains alpha decay, lets the Sun's protons fuse at lower temperatures than expected, and is
used in flash memory and the scanning tunnelling microscope.

## Superposition and quantum computers
Superposition means a quantum system can be in a combination of several states at once until it is measured. A qubit,
the basic unit of a quantum computer, can be in a superposition of 0 and 1, and n qubits together can represent 2ⁿ
states. Quantum computers use superposition and entanglement to solve some problems, such as simulating molecules,
much faster than ordinary computers, but they are still very sensitive to noise.
