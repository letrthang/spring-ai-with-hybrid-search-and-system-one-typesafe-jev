# Biotech basics — microscopy

## What a light microscope does
A light microscope uses visible light and glass lenses to make small objects look bigger. A typical lab microscope
magnifies 40x to 1,000x, enough to see cells and bacteria. Light passes through a thin sample from below, and the
lenses above form an enlarged image.

## Total magnification
Total magnification is the eyepiece magnification multiplied by the objective magnification. Most eyepieces are 10x,
and common objectives are 4x, 10x, 40x and 100x. So a 40x objective gives 400x in total, and a 100x objective gives
1,000x.

## Resolution
Resolution is the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as separate. For a light microscope it
is about 200 nm, roughly half the wavelength of visible light. Magnifying beyond this limit makes the image bigger but
not sharper, which is called empty magnification.

## Oil immersion
Oil immersion is a method that uses a drop of special oil between the slide and a 100x objective to improve
resolution. The oil has about the same refractive index as glass, around 1.5, so light is not bent away as it would
be in air. More light enters the lens, giving a sharper image at 1,000x.

## Focusing a microscope
Start focusing with the lowest-power objective and the stage close to the lens. Use the coarse focus knob to find the
sample, then the fine focus knob to sharpen it, and only then switch to a higher objective. With the 40x and 100x
objectives, use only fine focus to avoid crushing the slide.

## Staining samples
Most cells are nearly transparent, so stains are used to add colour and contrast. Methylene blue and crystal violet
stain bacteria, while haematoxylin colours cell nuclei blue-purple and eosin colours the cytoplasm pink in tissue
sections. Staining usually kills the cells, so living cells are viewed in other ways.

## Phase contrast microscopy
Phase contrast microscopy makes clear, unstained living cells visible. It turns tiny delays in light passing through
different parts of the cell into differences in brightness. This lets you watch living cells in culture, for example
to check their shape and how full the flask is, without staining them.

## Fluorescence microscopy
Fluorescence microscopy uses dyes or proteins that absorb light of one colour and give off light of a longer
wavelength. A filter lets only the emitted light reach the eye or camera, so labelled structures glow against a dark
background. Green fluorescent protein (GFP), for example, absorbs blue light at about 488 nm and glows green.

## Confocal microscopy
A confocal microscope scans a laser across the sample and uses a tiny pinhole to block out-of-focus light. This gives
sharp optical slices of thick samples, each often less than 1 µm thick. Stacking many slices lets a computer build a
three-dimensional picture of cells or tissue.

## Electron microscopy
An electron microscope uses a beam of electrons instead of light, giving a resolution down to about 0.1–1 nm. A
transmission electron microscope (TEM) shows the inside of very thin sections, while a scanning electron microscope
(SEM) shows surfaces in 3D. Samples must be placed in a vacuum, so living cells cannot be imaged.
