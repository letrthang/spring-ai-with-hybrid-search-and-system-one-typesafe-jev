# Physics basics — momentum and collisions

## What momentum is
Momentum is the quantity of motion an object has, equal to mass times velocity: p = m · v, measured in kg·m/s.
Momentum is a vector, so its direction matters as well as its size. A 1,500 kg car at 20 m/s has 30,000 kg·m/s of
momentum, while a 0.145 kg baseball thrown at 40 m/s has only 5.8 kg·m/s.

## Conservation of momentum
The law of conservation of momentum says the total momentum of a group of objects stays the same as long as no
outside force acts on them. In a collision or explosion the objects push on each other with equal and opposite
forces, so whatever momentum one gains, the other loses. The rule holds in every collision, whether the objects
bounce apart or stick together.

## Impulse
Impulse is the change in an object's momentum caused by a force acting for a time: J = F · Δt = Δp, measured in
newton-seconds (N·s). A tennis ball of about 0.06 kg arriving at 20 m/s and hit back the other way at 30 m/s
changes its momentum by 0.06 × 50 = 3 N·s. If the racket contact lasts 0.005 s, the average force is 3 / 0.005 =
600 N.

## Why longer impact times reduce force
For a given change in momentum, stretching out the impact time reduces the average force, because F = Δp / Δt. Car
airbags and crumple zones make a crash stop last longer, so the force on the passengers is smaller; doubling the
stopping time halves the average force. Bending your knees when landing from a jump and the padding inside helmets
work in the same way.

## Elastic collisions
An elastic collision is one in which both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved, so the objects bounce apart
with no energy lost. Collisions between hard steel balls, billiard balls and gas molecules are close to elastic.
When two equal masses collide head-on elastically, they swap velocities, which is why the moving ball in Newton's
cradle stops and the ball at the far end flies out.

## Inelastic collisions
An inelastic collision is one in which momentum is conserved but some kinetic energy is not, because part of it
turns into heat, sound and bending of the objects. Most everyday collisions are inelastic: a car crash, a football
hitting a wall or a dropped ball that bounces lower each time. The energy that disappears from motion still exists,
but in other forms.

## Perfectly inelastic collisions
A perfectly inelastic collision is one in which the objects stick together and move off with one shared velocity: v
= (m1 · v1 + m2 · v2) / (m1 + m2). If a 1,000 kg car at 20 m/s hits an identical parked car and they lock together,
they move off at 10 m/s. Momentum is unchanged, but half of the 200,000 J of kinetic energy is lost to crumpling
metal, heat and sound.

## Recoil
Recoil is the backward motion of an object that pushes something else forward, caused by conservation of momentum.
A rifle and bullet start at rest with zero total momentum, so the forward momentum of the bullet must be balanced
by backward momentum of the rifle. A 0.01 kg bullet leaving at 800 m/s carries 8 kg·m/s, so a 4 kg rifle recoils at
8 / 4 = 2 m/s.

## Rocket propulsion
A rocket moves forward by throwing exhaust gas backward at high speed, gaining forward momentum equal to the
backward momentum of the gas. Rocket thrust equals the mass of exhaust expelled per second times the exhaust speed
v_e: F = (Δm / Δt) · v_e. Burning 1,000 kg of propellant per second with v_e = 3,000 m/s gives 3,000,000 N of
thrust, and this works in empty space because a rocket needs no air to push against.

## Collisions in two dimensions
In a collision in two dimensions, momentum is conserved separately along each direction, for example east–west and
north–south. Physicists split each velocity into x and y parts, apply conservation to each part and then combine
them again. When a moving billiard ball strikes an identical ball at rest off-centre in an elastic collision, the
two balls always move away at 90° to each other.
