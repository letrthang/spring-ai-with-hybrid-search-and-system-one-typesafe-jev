# Biotech basics — stem cells

## What a stem cell is
A stem cell is an unspecialised cell that can both copy itself and turn into specialised cell types. Copying itself
is called self-renewal, and turning into a specialised cell is called differentiation. Many stem cells divide
asymmetrically, so one daughter cell stays a stem cell while the other starts to specialise, which keeps the stem
cell pool steady.

## Levels of potency
Potency describes how many cell types a stem cell can produce. A totipotent cell, such as the fertilised egg, can
form the whole embryo plus the placenta, while a pluripotent cell can form every cell type of the body but not the
placenta. A multipotent cell makes only the cells of one family, such as blood cells, and a unipotent cell makes just
one cell type.

## Embryonic stem cells
Embryonic stem cells are pluripotent cells taken from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst, an early embryo about 5
days old. Human embryonic stem cell lines were first grown in 1998, and in the lab they can divide indefinitely while
staying pluripotent. The use of embryonic stem cells is debated because obtaining them destroys the embryo, so the
rules differ from country to country.

## Adult stem cells
Adult stem cells, also called tissue stem cells, are multipotent cells that repair and renew tissues throughout life.
Haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow make all blood cells, hundreds of billions every day, and intestinal
stem cells replace the gut lining about every 3–5 days. Adult stem cells are rare in the body and are harder to
expand in culture than embryonic stem cells.

## Induced pluripotent stem cells
Induced pluripotent stem cells, or iPSCs, are ordinary adult cells reprogrammed back into a pluripotent state. In
2006 Shinya Yamanaka showed that switching on four genes, Oct4, Sox2, Klf4 and c-Myc, turns mouse skin fibroblasts
into embryonic-like stem cells, and the method worked for human cells in 2007. iPSCs avoid the use of embryos and can
be made from the patient's own cells, and Yamanaka shared the 2012 Nobel Prize for the discovery.

## Directed differentiation
Directed differentiation is the lab process of guiding pluripotent stem cells into a chosen cell type. Scientists add
growth factors and small molecules in a timed sequence that mimics the signals of the developing embryo, for example
activin A to start forming endoderm. Protocols lasting days to weeks can produce beating heart muscle cells, neurons,
liver cells or insulin-making beta cells.

## Growing stem cells in the lab
Pluripotent stem cells are grown as tight, flat colonies on a coated surface such as Matrigel or vitronectin, in a
defined medium that is changed every day. The cultures are kept at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 and passaged every 4–7 days,
before the colonies grow too large. Staff check pluripotency markers such as OCT4 and SSEA-4 and remove colonies that
start to differentiate on their own.

## Blood stem cell transplants
A haematopoietic stem cell transplant replaces a damaged blood-forming system with healthy blood stem cells. Doctors
collect the cells from bone marrow, from blood after the drug G-CSF moves them out of the marrow, or from umbilical
cord blood, and infuse them into a vein after chemotherapy. Blood stem cell transplants have been performed since the
late 1960s to treat leukaemia, lymphoma and inherited blood disorders, and are the most established stem cell
therapy.

## Organoids
An organoid is a tiny three-dimensional tissue grown from stem cells that copies some of the structure and function
of a real organ. Stem cells embedded in a gel such as Matrigel organise themselves into mini-guts, mini-livers or
brain organoids a few millimetres across. Organoids let researchers study disease and test drugs on human tissue, and
organoids grown from a patient's tumour can help predict how that tumour responds to treatment.

## Risks of stem cell therapy
Stem cell therapy carries specific safety risks that regulators review carefully. Leftover undifferentiated
pluripotent cells can form tumours called teratomas, donor cells can be rejected by the immune system, and
reprogramming genes such as c-Myc may promote cancer. Some clinics sell unproven stem cell treatments, so patients
should look for therapies tested in registered clinical trials and approved by regulators.
