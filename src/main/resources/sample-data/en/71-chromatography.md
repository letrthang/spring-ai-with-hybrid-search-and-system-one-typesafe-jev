# Biotech basics — chromatography

## What chromatography does
Chromatography separates the molecules in a mixture by how strongly each one interacts with two phases. The mixture
is carried by a moving liquid or gas, called the mobile phase, past a fixed material, called the stationary phase.
Molecules that stick more to the stationary phase move more slowly, so the components leave at different times and
can be collected separately.

## Paper and thin-layer chromatography
Paper chromatography and thin-layer chromatography (TLC) are the simplest forms of chromatography. A small spot of
sample is placed near the bottom of a paper strip or a silica-coated plate, and a solvent creeps upward by capillary
action, carrying each compound a different distance. The distance a spot travels divided by the distance the solvent
front travels is called the Rf value, which is always between 0 and 1.

## Column chromatography
Column chromatography passes the sample through a tube packed with small beads that act as the stationary phase.
Buffer flows through the column, and the liquid coming out, called the eluate, is collected in a series of tubes
called fractions. In lab-scale protein purification a column holding 1 to 5 mL of resin is common, while industrial
columns can hold hundreds of litres.

## Size-exclusion chromatography
Size-exclusion chromatography, also called gel filtration, separates molecules by size using porous beads. Small
molecules enter the pores and take a longer path, so they leave the column later, while large molecules cannot enter
the pores and leave first. Size-exclusion chromatography is often the last step of protein purification and is also
used to move a protein into a new buffer or to check for clumps called aggregates.

## Ion-exchange chromatography
Ion-exchange chromatography separates molecules by their electric charge. An anion exchanger carries positive groups
and binds negatively charged proteins, while a cation exchanger carries negative groups and binds positively charged
proteins. Bound proteins are released by slowly raising the salt concentration, for example from 0 to 1 M NaCl, and
weakly bound proteins come off before strongly bound ones.

## Affinity chromatography
Affinity chromatography captures one protein using a specific binding partner fixed to the beads. A common example is
the His-tag: a protein made with six histidines at one end binds to nickel ions on Ni-NTA resin and is released with
about 250 mM imidazole. Protein A resin, which binds the Fc region of antibodies, is the standard first step for
purifying therapeutic monoclonal antibodies.

## Reversed-phase chromatography
Reversed-phase chromatography separates molecules by how hydrophobic they are. The stationary phase is silica coated
with long carbon chains such as C18, and the mobile phase starts watery and becomes more organic, usually by adding
acetonitrile. Reversed-phase chromatography is the most common mode for small molecules and peptides, and it is often
connected directly to a mass spectrometer.

## HPLC
HPLC, high-performance liquid chromatography, pushes the mobile phase through a column of very small particles at
high pressure. Particles of 2 to 5 µm give sharp, well-separated peaks but need pressures of roughly 100 to 400 bar,
and UHPLC systems with particles below 2 µm run at up to about 1,000 bar. HPLC is widely used to check the purity of
drugs and to measure how much of a compound is in a sample.

## Reading a chromatogram
A chromatogram is the graph of detector signal against time that a chromatography run produces. Each compound appears
as a peak, and the time at which its peak appears is called the retention time, which helps identify the compound.
The area under a peak is proportional to the amount of compound, so comparing peak areas with known standards gives
concentrations.

## Detectors in chromatography
Detectors in chromatography sense each compound as it leaves the column. A UV detector is the most common, and
proteins are usually followed at 280 nm, where the amino acids tryptophan and tyrosine absorb light. Other options
include fluorescence detectors, conductivity detectors that track salt gradients, and mass spectrometers that also
reveal the mass of each compound.
