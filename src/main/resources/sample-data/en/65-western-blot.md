# Biotech basics — Western blot

## What a Western blot shows
A Western blot shows whether a specific protein is present in a sample, how large it is and roughly how much there
is. The method combines three steps: separating proteins by size with SDS-PAGE, transferring them to a membrane,
and detecting the target with antibodies. The name is a play on the Southern blot for DNA, and the Western blot was
first described in 1979.

## Preparing the sample
Sample preparation for a Western blot starts by breaking open cells in a lysis buffer such as RIPA, which contains
detergents that release the proteins. Protease inhibitors are added and the sample is kept on ice to stop the
proteins from being broken down. The total protein is then measured, often with a BCA or Bradford assay, so that
the same amount, typically 10–50 µg, can be loaded in every lane.

## Denaturing and loading
Before loading, Western blot samples are mixed with Laemmli buffer, which contains SDS, a reducing agent such as
DTT or β-mercaptoethanol, glycerol and a blue tracking dye. Heating at 95 °C for about 5 minutes unfolds the
proteins and breaks disulfide bonds, so each protein runs according to its mass. A pre-stained protein ladder is
loaded in one lane to show sizes in kilodaltons (kDa).

## Choosing the gel percentage
The polyacrylamide percentage of the gel sets which protein sizes separate best in a Western blot. Gels of 6–8%
suit large proteins above about 100 kDa, 10–12% gels suit the common range of 20–100 kDa, and 15% gels resolve
small proteins below 20 kDa. Gradient gels, for example 4–20%, cover a wide range in one run. Protein gels
typically run at 100–200 V for about 45–90 minutes.

## Transfer to a membrane
In the transfer step of a Western blot, an electric current moves the proteins out of the gel and onto a thin
membrane where antibodies can reach them. The gel and membrane are pressed together in a sandwich of sponges and
filter paper, and in wet transfer the stack sits in buffer at about 100 V for 1 hour, often in the cold. Semi-dry
and dry transfer systems are faster and take about 7–30 minutes.

## PVDF and nitrocellulose membranes
The two common Western blot membranes are nitrocellulose and PVDF. Nitrocellulose is easy to use and gives low
background, while PVDF is tougher, binds more protein and can be stripped and re-probed, but PVDF must first be
wetted in methanol because it repels water. A pore size of 0.45 µm suits most proteins and 0.2 µm keeps small
proteins from passing through, and a quick Ponceau S stain shows red bands that confirm the transfer worked.

## Blocking the membrane
Blocking covers the empty parts of a Western blot membrane so that antibodies stick only to the target protein. The
membrane is soaked for about 1 hour in a protein solution, usually 5% non-fat dry milk or 3–5% bovine serum albumin
(BSA) in TBS-T, a buffer with the detergent Tween 20. BSA is preferred when detecting phosphorylated proteins,
because milk contains the phosphoprotein casein, which raises the background.

## Primary and secondary antibodies
A Western blot normally uses two antibodies. The primary antibody binds the target protein and is often incubated
overnight at 4 °C at a dilution of about 1:500 to 1:5,000; after washing, a secondary antibody that recognises
antibodies from the primary's host species, such as anti-rabbit IgG, is added for about 1 hour. The secondary
antibody carries an enzyme or a fluorescent dye, and because several secondary antibodies can bind one primary, the
signal is amplified.

## Detecting the signal
Western blot signals are most often detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL). The secondary antibody carries
the enzyme horseradish peroxidase (HRP), which reacts with a luminol substrate to give off light where the target
protein sits, and the light is captured by a CCD camera imager or X-ray film. Fluorescent secondary antibodies are
an alternative that can detect two proteins at once in different colours and give a wider linear range for
quantification.

## Loading controls and reading the blot
A loading control is a protein whose level should be the same in every sample, used to check that equal amounts
were loaded on a Western blot. Common loading controls are β-actin (42 kDa), GAPDH (about 36 kDa) and α-tubulin
(about 50–55 kDa), or a total protein stain. The target band intensity is divided by the loading control intensity
to compare samples, and extra bands may come from protein breakdown, modifications or antibody cross-reactivity.
