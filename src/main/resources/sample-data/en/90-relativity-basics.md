# Physics basics — relativity

## The principle of relativity
The principle of relativity says that the laws of physics are the same for every observer moving at a constant
velocity. A passenger in a smooth train or aircraft cannot tell, by any experiment inside, whether the vehicle is
moving steadily or standing still. Galileo described this idea in 1632 using a ship, and Albert Einstein built his
special theory of relativity on it in 1905.

## The constant speed of light
The speed of light in a vacuum, c = 299,792,458 m/s, is the same for every observer, however fast the observer or the
light source is moving. A light beam from a spaceship moving at half the speed of light still travels at c, not 1.5 c,
as measured from the ground. The Michelson–Morley experiment of 1887 found no change in the speed of light with
Earth's motion.

## Time dilation
Time dilation means that a moving clock runs slower than a clock at rest, as measured by the observer at rest. The
stretched time is t = γ t₀, where the Lorentz factor is γ = 1 / √(1 − v² / c²). At 60% of the speed of light γ = 1.25,
so 1 hour on a passing spaceship lasts 1.25 hours for observers on Earth, and at 99% of the speed of light γ is about
7.1.

## Muons and time dilation
Muons from the sky are clear evidence for time dilation. Cosmic rays create muons about 15 km up, and a muon at rest
lives on average only 2.2 µs, long enough to travel about 660 m even at nearly the speed of light. Muons at 99.5% of
the speed of light have γ = 10, so their clocks run 10 times slower and many of them reach the ground.

## Length contraction
Length contraction means that a moving object is measured to be shorter along its direction of motion, L = L₀ / γ. A
spaceship 100 m long at rest, passing at 60% of the speed of light, is measured from Earth to be only 80 m long. Its
width and height do not change, and the effect is far too small to notice at everyday speeds.

## Relativity of simultaneity
The relativity of simultaneity means that two events happening at the same time for one observer can happen at
different times for another observer who is moving. If lightning strikes both ends of a fast train at the same moment
for someone on the platform, a passenger in the middle of the train sees the front strike first. Neither observer is
wrong, because time order of distant events depends on motion.

## Mass–energy equivalence
Mass–energy equivalence, written E = m c², says that mass is a form of energy and that a small mass equals a huge
amount of energy. One kilogram of mass corresponds to 9 × 10¹⁶ J, and just 1 g corresponds to about 9 × 10¹³ J,
roughly the energy of 21,000 tonnes of TNT. Nuclear reactors and the Sun release energy because their products have
slightly less mass than the starting nuclei.

## The cosmic speed limit
No object with mass can reach the speed of light, because its kinetic energy would become infinite. As an object
speeds up, each extra bit of speed costs more and more energy, so the speed only creeps closer to c. Protons in the
Large Hadron Collider carry 6.8 TeV of energy, about 7,000 times their rest energy, yet move at only about 99.999999%
of the speed of light.

## General relativity and curved spacetime
General relativity, published by Einstein in 1915, describes gravity as the curving of space and time by mass and
energy. Light passing the edge of the Sun is bent by 1.75 arcseconds, which was measured during the solar eclipse of
1919. General relativity also predicted gravitational waves, ripples in spacetime that the LIGO detectors first
observed in 2015 from two merging black holes.

## GPS and relativity
GPS satellites must correct their clocks for both special and general relativity to give accurate positions. Their
high speed makes the clocks run about 7 µs a day slow, while the weaker gravity 20,200 km up makes them run about 45
µs a day fast, a net gain of about 38 µs a day. Without this correction, GPS positions would drift by roughly 10 km
every day.
