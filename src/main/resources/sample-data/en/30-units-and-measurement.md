# Physics basics — units and measurement

## The SI base units
The International System of Units (SI) has seven base units: the metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole and
candela. Every other unit, such as the newton or the joule, is built from these seven. Scientists everywhere use the
same units, so results from different labs and countries can be compared directly.

## Metric prefixes
Metric prefixes scale a unit by powers of ten. Kilo means a thousand (10³), mega a million (10⁶), milli a thousandth
(10⁻³), micro a millionth (10⁻⁶) and nano a billionth (10⁻⁹). So 1 km is 1,000 m, and 1 µm is 0.000001 m, far thinner
than a human hair, which is about 70 µm wide.

## Converting units
To convert units, multiply by a conversion factor equal to one, such as 1,000 m / 1 km. A speed of 72 km/h becomes 72
× 1,000 m ÷ 3,600 s = 20 m/s. A quick rule: divide km/h by 3.6 to get m/s, and multiply m/s by 3.6 to get km/h.

## Significant figures
Significant figures show how precisely a number is known. A length written as 2.50 m has three significant figures and
implies it was measured to the nearest centimetre, while 2.5 m implies only the nearest ten centimetres. A calculated
result should not be quoted with more significant figures than the least precise measurement used.

## Accuracy and precision
Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value; precision is how close repeated measurements are to each
other. A scale that always reads 2.0 kg too heavy is precise but not accurate. Calibrating an instrument against a
known standard fixes accuracy, while careful technique and better instruments improve precision.

## Random and systematic errors
A random error makes repeated readings scatter above and below the true value, for example when a stopwatch is started
by hand. A systematic error shifts every reading in the same direction, such as a ruler with a worn end. Averaging
many readings reduces random error but does nothing to remove a systematic one.

## Measurement uncertainty
Every measurement has an uncertainty, written as a ± value, such as 25.3 ± 0.1 cm. For a simple ruler the uncertainty
is usually taken as half the smallest division, so a millimetre ruler gives ±0.5 mm. The percentage uncertainty is the
uncertainty divided by the measured value, times 100.

## Scientific notation
Scientific notation writes a number as a value between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of ten. The speed of light,
300,000,000 m/s, becomes 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s, and the mass of an electron is 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg. It keeps very large and very
small numbers short and makes the number of significant figures clear.

## Dimensional analysis
Dimensional analysis checks an equation by comparing the units on both sides. Distance = speed × time works because
metres per second multiplied by seconds gives metres. If the units on the two sides do not match, the equation must be
wrong, which makes this a fast way to catch mistakes in a calculation.

## Mass and weight
Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms, and it is the same everywhere. Weight is the force
of gravity on that mass, W = m · g, measured in newtons. A 60 kg person weighs about 590 N on Earth but only about 97
N on the Moon, where g is 1.62 m/s².
