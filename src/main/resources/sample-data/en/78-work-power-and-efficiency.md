# Physics basics — work, power and efficiency

## What work means in physics
In physics, work is done when a force moves an object along the direction of the force: W = F · d. Work is measured
in joules, and 1 J is the work done when a force of 1 N moves something 1 m. Pushing hard on a wall that does not
move does no work on the wall, however tiring it feels, because the distance moved is zero.

## Work done lifting an object
The work done lifting an object at steady speed equals its weight times the height raised: W = m · g · h. Lifting a
10 kg box onto a shelf 2 m high takes 10 × 9.8 × 2 = 196 J. That work is stored as gravitational potential energy
and comes back out if the box falls.

## Force at an angle
When a force acts at an angle θ to the motion, only the part of the force along the motion does work: W = F · d ·
cos θ. Pulling a sled 10 m with a 100 N rope tilted at 60° does 100 × 10 × 0.5 = 500 J of work. A force at 90° to
the motion, such as gravity on a car driving on flat ground, does no work, and a force against the motion, such as
friction, does negative work.

## The work–energy theorem
The work–energy theorem says the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy: W_net = ½ m v² − ½
m u². To speed a 1,000 kg car from rest to 20 m/s, the net force must do ½ × 1,000 × 20² = 200,000 J of work. The
brakes do the same amount of negative work to stop the car, turning that energy into heat.

## What power is
Power is the rate of doing work or transferring energy: P = W / t, measured in watts, where 1 W = 1 J per second. A
60 kg person climbing 3 m of stairs does about 60 × 9.8 × 3 ≈ 1,760 J of work; doing it in 4 s takes about 440 W,
while walking up in 10 s takes about 176 W. The same work done faster needs more power.

## Power and speed
For an object moving at a steady speed, power equals force times velocity: P = F · v. A car cruising at 25 m/s (90
km/h) against 600 N of air drag and rolling resistance needs 600 × 25 = 15,000 W, or 15 kW, at the wheels. Because
air drag grows roughly with the square of speed, the power needed rises roughly with the cube of speed.

## Horsepower and kilowatts
Horsepower (hp) is an old unit of power still used for engines, and 1 hp is about 746 W. James Watt defined
horsepower in the 1780s by estimating how fast a horse could haul coal out of a mine. A car engine rated at 100 kW
therefore produces about 134 hp, and a fit cyclist can sustain around 250 W, roughly a third of a horsepower.

## Energy from power and time
Energy equals power multiplied by time: E = P · t. Electricity companies bill energy in kilowatt-hours, where 1 kWh
is 1,000 W used for one hour, or 3.6 million joules. A 2 kW heater running for 3 hours uses 2 × 3 = 6 kWh, while a
10 W LED bulb would need 600 hours to use the same amount.

## Efficiency of machines
The efficiency of a machine is the share of input energy or power that it turns into useful output: efficiency =
useful output / total input × 100%. A large electric motor can reach about 95%, a petrol car engine about 25–35%
and human muscles about 20–25%, with the rest lost as heat. When devices are chained, their efficiencies multiply,
so a 90% motor driving an 80% pump gives 72% overall.

## Simple machines and mechanical advantage
Simple machines such as levers, pulleys and ramps let a small force move a large load by trading force for
distance. Mechanical advantage is the load divided by the effort: MA = load / effort. A pulley system with MA = 4
lifts a 400 N load with a 100 N pull, but the rope must be pulled 4 m for every 1 m the load rises, so the work put
in is never less than the work got out.
