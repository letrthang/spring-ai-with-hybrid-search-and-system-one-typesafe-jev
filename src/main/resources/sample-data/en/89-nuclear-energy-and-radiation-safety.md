# Physics basics — nuclear energy and radiation safety

## How a nuclear power station works
A nuclear power station makes electricity by using the heat from fission in a reactor to boil water into steam. The
steam spins a turbine connected to a generator, just as in a coal or gas power station, and is then cooled back into
water and reused. A typical large reactor produces about 3,000 MW of heat and 1,000 MW of electricity, an efficiency
of about 33%.

## Nuclear fuel and enrichment
Nuclear fuel for most power reactors is uranium dioxide pressed into small ceramic pellets and sealed in long metal
fuel rods. Natural uranium contains only about 0.7% uranium-235, the isotope that splits easily, so it is enriched to
about 3 to 5% for power reactors. A single fuel pellet, about the size of a fingertip, releases roughly as much energy
as 1 tonne of coal.

## The moderator
The moderator in a nuclear reactor is a material that slows down the fast neutrons released by fission. Slow neutrons,
called thermal neutrons, are far more likely to be absorbed by uranium-235 and cause the next fission. Ordinary water,
heavy water and graphite are common moderators, and in most reactors the same water also acts as the coolant that
carries heat away from the core.

## Decay heat and reactor cooling
Decay heat is the heat that a reactor core keeps producing after shutdown because its radioactive fission products
continue to decay. Just after shutdown decay heat is about 6 to 7% of full power and it is still around 1% after a few
hours, so the core must be cooled for days. In 2011 the Fukushima reactors lost power to their cooling pumps after a
tsunami, and the overheated fuel melted.

## Radioactive waste
Radioactive waste from nuclear power ranges from low-level waste, such as used gloves and tools, to high-level waste,
mainly spent fuel. Spent fuel is first kept in water-filled cooling pools for several years and then moved to sealed
steel and concrete dry casks. For final disposal, countries such as Finland are building deep geological repositories
that bury the waste around 400 m down in stable bedrock.

## Time, distance and shielding
Time, distance and shielding are the three basic ways to reduce a dose from a radiation source. Halving the time spent
near a source halves the dose, and doubling the distance from a small source cuts the dose rate to a quarter because
of the inverse square law. Shielding blocks radiation: paper stops alpha, a few millimetres of aluminium stops beta,
and lead or thick concrete reduces gamma.

## Contamination and irradiation
Irradiation means being exposed to radiation from a source outside the body, while contamination means radioactive
material has landed on the skin or got inside the body. An irradiated person or object does not become radioactive, so
food sterilised with gamma rays is safe to eat. Contamination is harder to control, because the material keeps
emitting radiation until it is washed off or leaves the body.

## Radiation dose limits
Radiation dose limits set the maximum extra dose a person should receive from human-made sources, on top of natural
background. The International Commission on Radiological Protection recommends a limit of 1 mSv a year for members of
the public. Radiation workers may receive up to 20 mSv a year averaged over five years, with no more than 50 mSv in
any single year, and medical doses to patients are not counted.

## Health effects of radiation
High doses of radiation received quickly cause radiation sickness, while low doses slightly raise the long-term risk
of cancer. Symptoms such as nausea and a drop in blood cells appear above about 1 Sv, and a dose of about 4 to 5 Sv
kills roughly half of people without medical treatment. At low doses the extra lifetime cancer risk is estimated at
about 5% per sievert, or about 0.005% per mSv.

## Measuring radiation
Radiation is measured with instruments such as Geiger–Müller counters, which click each time a particle or gamma ray
ionises the gas inside the tube. Background radiation usually gives a dose rate of about 0.1 to 0.3 µSv per hour.
Radiation workers wear personal dosimeters, such as film badges or electronic dosimeters, which record the total dose
they receive over weeks or months.
