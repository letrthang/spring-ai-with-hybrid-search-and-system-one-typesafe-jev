# Biotech basics — biosensors

## What a biosensor is
A biosensor is a device that uses a biological recognition element to detect a specific substance and turns that
detection into a measurable signal. Every biosensor has three parts: a bioreceptor such as an enzyme, antibody or DNA
strand, a transducer that converts the binding event into an electrical or optical signal, and electronics that read
and display the result. The substance being measured is called the analyte.

## Bioreceptors
The bioreceptor is the part of a biosensor that recognises the analyte, and the bioreceptor determines how selective
the sensor is. Enzymes recognise their substrate and turn it into a product, antibodies bind antigens, single DNA
strands bind complementary strands, and aptamers are short DNA or RNA strands selected in the lab to bind a chosen
target. Whole cells can also act as bioreceptors, for example in sensors that detect toxic chemicals in water.

## Electrochemical biosensors
Electrochemical biosensors measure an electrical signal, such as a current or a voltage, that changes when the
analyte is present. In an amperometric sensor the reaction at an electrode produces a current proportional to the
analyte concentration, while potentiometric sensors measure a voltage and impedance sensors measure changes in
resistance to alternating current. Electrochemical biosensors are cheap, small and easy to mass-produce on printed
electrodes.

## The blood glucose meter
The blood glucose meter is the most widely used biosensor in the world. A disposable test strip holds the enzyme
glucose oxidase or glucose dehydrogenase, which reacts with glucose in a drop of blood of about 1 µL or less, and the
electrons released create a current that the meter converts into a glucose reading within seconds. Normal fasting
blood glucose is about 4 to 5.5 mmol/L, or roughly 70 to 100 mg/dL.

## Continuous glucose monitors
A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) is a wearable biosensor that measures glucose every few minutes for days at a
time. A thin filament coated with glucose oxidase sits under the skin and measures glucose in the interstitial fluid
between cells, which follows blood glucose with a delay of about 5 to 15 minutes. CGM readings are sent wirelessly to
a phone or reader that can warn about high or low glucose, and one sensor is typically worn for 10 to 14 days.

## Optical biosensors
Optical biosensors detect the analyte through a change in light, such as absorbance, fluorescence, luminescence or
refractive index. A colour change can be read by eye or by a camera, while fluorescence-based biosensors can detect
very low concentrations because only the labelled molecules glow. Fibre-optic and smartphone-based optical biosensors
are being developed for testing outside the lab.

## Surface plasmon resonance
Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) is a label-free optical method that measures molecules binding to a thin gold film in
real time. One binding partner is fixed on the gold surface, and when the other partner binds, the refractive index
near the surface changes and shifts the angle at which light is absorbed by the gold. SPR instruments record binding
and release over time, which gives the association rate, the dissociation rate and the binding strength, KD, of pairs
such as an antibody and its antigen.

## Lateral flow tests
A lateral flow test is a simple paper-based biosensor that gives a visible result in about 10 to 20 minutes without
any instrument. The liquid sample flows along a strip by capillary action, picks up antibodies labelled with coloured
gold nanoparticles, and is captured at a test line if the target is present. A separate control line must also appear
to show the test ran correctly; home pregnancy tests and rapid antigen tests for COVID-19 work this way.

## Sensitivity, selectivity and limit of detection
Sensitivity, selectivity and limit of detection describe how well a biosensor performs. Sensitivity is how much the
signal changes per unit change in analyte concentration, selectivity is how well the sensor ignores similar
molecules, and the limit of detection (LOD) is the lowest concentration that can be reliably told apart from a blank
sample. The LOD is often estimated as the blank signal plus three times its standard deviation.

## Calibration and drift
Calibration and drift decide whether a biosensor's readings stay accurate over time. Calibration links the sensor
signal to known concentrations, often with two or more standard solutions, while drift is a slow change in the signal
caused by the bioreceptor losing activity, fouling of the surface or temperature changes. Enzyme-based biosensors are
especially sensitive to temperature and storage conditions, so many have an expiry date and built-in temperature
correction.
