# Biotech basics — making monoclonal antibodies

## Why monoclonal antibodies are made
Monoclonal antibodies are made to obtain unlimited amounts of one identical antibody against a chosen target. Every
molecule in a monoclonal batch binds the same epitope with the same strength, which makes diagnostic tests and
medicines reproducible. The hybridoma method that first made monoclonal antibodies possible was invented by Georges
Köhler and César Milstein in 1975 and earned the 1984 Nobel Prize.

## Immunising a mouse
Monoclonal antibody production by the hybridoma method starts by immunising a mouse with the target antigen. The
antigen is injected several times over a few weeks, often mixed with an adjuvant, so that B cells making antibodies
against the target multiply. Once blood tests show a strong antibody titre, the spleen is removed as a rich source of
these B cells.

## Myeloma cells
Myeloma cells are cancerous B cells that grow forever in culture and give a hybridoma its immortality. The myeloma
lines used for fusion, such as Sp2/0 or NS0, were selected so that they make no antibody of their own. These myeloma
lines also lack the enzyme HGPRT, which makes them sensitive to HAT medium so that unfused myeloma cells can be
killed later.

## Making hybridomas by cell fusion
A hybridoma is a hybrid cell formed by fusing an antibody-making B cell with a myeloma cell. Spleen cells and myeloma
cells are mixed and treated with polyethylene glycol (PEG) or a short electric pulse, which makes the membranes of
neighbouring cells merge. The resulting hybridoma inherits the antibody genes of the B cell and the endless growth of
the myeloma cell.

## HAT selection
HAT selection is the culture step that keeps only hybridomas alive after fusion. HAT medium contains hypoxanthine,
aminopterin and thymidine; aminopterin blocks the main pathway for making DNA building blocks, so cells must rely on
a salvage pathway that needs HGPRT. Unfused myeloma cells lack HGPRT and die, unfused B cells die naturally within
about two weeks, and only hybridomas survive.

## Screening and cloning hybridomas
After HAT selection, hybridoma culture supernatants are screened, usually by ELISA, to find the wells that make
antibody against the target. Positive wells are then cloned by limiting dilution, in which cells are diluted to an
average of less than one cell per well of a 96-well plate. Limiting dilution is repeated so that each final cell line
descends from a single cell and secretes one monoclonal antibody.

## Chimeric, humanised and human antibodies
Mouse monoclonal antibodies given to patients often trigger an immune reaction called a HAMA response, so therapeutic
antibodies are engineered to look human. A chimeric antibody joins mouse variable regions to human constant regions
and is about two-thirds human, while a humanised antibody keeps only the mouse CDR loops and is over 90 % human.
Older drug names show the type: -ximab for chimeric such as rituximab, -zumab for humanised such as trastuzumab, and
-umab for fully human such as adalimumab.

## Phage display
Phage display is a method for finding antibodies without immunising an animal. Libraries of billions of different
antibody fragments are displayed on the surface of filamentous bacteriophages, and the phages that stick to an
immobilised target are kept and amplified over several rounds. Adalimumab, approved in 2002, was the first fully
human antibody drug discovered by phage display, and Greg Winter and George Smith shared the 2018 Nobel Prize for the
technique.

## Producing antibodies in CHO cells
Therapeutic monoclonal antibodies are mostly produced in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells rather than in hybridomas.
The genes for the antibody heavy and light chains are inserted into CHO cells, and a high-producing clone is selected
and grown in fed-batch bioreactors for about 10–14 days. Modern CHO processes reach titres of 3–10 g of antibody per
litre, and CHO cells add sugar chains similar to human ones that the antibody needs to work well.

## Monoclonal antibodies as medicines
Monoclonal antibodies are one of the largest classes of medicines, with more than 100 approved worldwide. Trastuzumab
blocks the HER2 receptor in some breast cancers, rituximab removes B cells in lymphoma, adalimumab neutralises the
inflammatory protein TNF in rheumatoid arthritis, and pembrolizumab releases a brake on T cells so they attack
tumours. A full antibody is a large protein of about 150 kDa, so antibody drugs are given by injection or infusion
rather than as tablets.
