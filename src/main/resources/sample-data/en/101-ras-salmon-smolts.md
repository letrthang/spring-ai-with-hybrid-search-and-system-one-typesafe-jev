# RAS basics — Raising salmon smolts

## Why salmon smolts are raised in RAS
Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) smolts are now mostly raised in RAS because the farm controls temperature, light and
water quality all year. A RAS can produce large smolts or post-smolts of 100 to 500 g instead of the traditional 60
to 100 g smolt. Larger smolts spend fewer months in sea cages, which lowers their exposure to sea lice and other
risks at sea.

## What smoltification is
Smoltification is the change that turns a freshwater salmon parr into a smolt that can survive in sea water. During
smoltification the fish turns silver, loses its dark parr marks, becomes more slender and develops dark fin edges.
Inside, the gills increase the enzyme Na+/K+-ATPase so the fish can pump out the extra salt it takes in at sea.

## Using light to trigger smoltification
Salmon smolt farms use a light programme called the winter signal to trigger smoltification at the planned time. A
common programme gives about 6 weeks of short days, 12 hours light and 12 hours dark, followed by continuous light
(24 hours a day). The smolts are usually ready for sea water about 300 to 400 degree-days after the switch to
continuous light.

## Water temperature for salmon smolts
Salmon smolts in a RAS are usually kept at about 12 to 14 °C, which gives fast growth without harming smolt quality.
Temperatures above about 16 °C during smoltification can weaken seawater tolerance and raise the risk of disease.
Farms track development in degree-days, which is the water temperature in °C added up for each day.

## Water quality limits for smolts
Salmon smolts are more sensitive to poor water quality than most other RAS fish, especially to carbon dioxide. Smolt
farms aim to keep dissolved CO2 below about 10 to 15 mg/L, dissolved oxygen near 100 % saturation and TAN and nitrite
as low as possible. High CO2 in the freshwater phase is linked to kidney problems and poorer survival after transfer
to sea.

## Testing whether smolts are ready
Farms test smolt readiness with gill Na+/K+-ATPase measurements and seawater challenge tests. In a seawater challenge
test, a sample of fish is held in sea water of about 33 to 35 ppt for 72 hours and blood chloride is then measured.
Plasma chloride below about 150 mmol/L, together with a silver colour and good feeding, shows that the smolts are
ready.

## Post-smolts in brackish water
Many salmon farms grow post-smolts in RAS at brackish salinity of about 10 to 15 ppt before moving them to sea cages.
Brackish water lowers the energy the fish spend on salt balance, so growth is good and the move to full sea water is
gentler. Salinity is raised in steps of a few ppt per day so the fish and the biofilter can adjust.

## Stocking density for salmon smolts
Salmon smolts in a RAS are usually stocked at about 40 to 80 kg/m³, depending on oxygen supply, water quality and
local welfare rules. Higher densities give more fish per tank but cause more fin damage and stress if water quality
slips. Farms watch fin condition, skin damage and feeding behaviour as welfare indicators for smolt density.

## The smolt window and desmoltification
Salmon smolts can tolerate sea water only during a limited smolt window of a few weeks. If smolts stay too long in
fresh water, especially at warm temperatures, they desmoltify and lose their seawater tolerance again. Planning sea
transfer in degree-days after the light switch helps the farm deliver smolts inside this window.

## Transferring smolts to sea
Before transfer to sea, salmon smolts are vaccinated, usually at least about 500 degree-days ahead so the vaccine has
time to work. Feed is withheld for 1 to 2 days, and fish are moved gently with fish pumps into a wellboat or truck
with oxygenated water. Careful handling during transfer reduces scale loss and the losses that often follow in the
first weeks at sea.
