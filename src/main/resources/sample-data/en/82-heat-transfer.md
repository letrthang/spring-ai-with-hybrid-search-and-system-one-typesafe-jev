# Physics basics — heat transfer

## Three ways heat moves
Heat transfer is the movement of thermal energy from a hotter place to a colder one, and heat transfer happens in
three ways: conduction, convection and radiation. Conduction passes energy through materials by contact, convection
carries energy with a moving fluid, and radiation carries energy as infrared waves that need no medium. Most real
situations, such as a pan on a stove, combine all three.

## Thermal conductivity
Thermal conductivity, written k and measured in W/(m·K), tells how well a material conducts heat. Copper has a thermal
conductivity of about 400 W/(m·K), steel about 50, glass about 1, wood about 0.15 and still air only about 0.025.
Insulating materials such as foam, fibreglass and down feathers work mainly by trapping small pockets of still air.

## Calculating heat conduction
The rate of heat conduction through a flat layer is P = k · A · ΔT / d, where A is the area, ΔT the temperature
difference and d the thickness. A glass pane 1 m² in area and 4 mm thick, with k = 1 W/(m·K) and a 1 °C difference
across the glass, would conduct 250 W. In practice, thin layers of still air on each side of the glass cut the real
heat loss far below that figure.

## Why metal feels colder than wood
A metal bench feels colder than a wooden bench at the same temperature because metal conducts heat away from your skin
much faster. Your skin senses how quickly it loses heat, not the temperature of the object itself. On a hot day the
effect reverses, and sunlit metal feels hotter than wood because metal delivers heat to your skin more quickly.

## Natural and forced convection
Natural convection happens when density differences alone make a heated fluid rise, as with warm air rising from a
radiator. Forced convection uses a fan, a pump or the wind to push the fluid along, which carries heat away much
faster. Blowing on hot soup is forced convection, and so is wind chill, where a 30 km/h wind makes −5 °C air feel like
about −13 °C.

## The Stefan–Boltzmann law
The Stefan–Boltzmann law says the power radiated by a surface is P = ε · σ · A · T⁴, where σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴),
T is the absolute temperature in kelvin and ε is the emissivity. Emissivity runs from 0 to 1: about 0.95 for human
skin and matt black paint, and as low as 0.03 for polished aluminium. Doubling the absolute temperature makes a
surface radiate 16 times more power.

## Newton's law of cooling
Newton's law of cooling says an object cools at a rate proportional to the temperature difference between the object
and its surroundings. A cup of tea at 90 °C in a 20 °C room loses heat quickly at first, then more and more slowly as
the gap narrows. Newton's law of cooling explains why a hot drink falls from 90 °C to 70 °C much faster than from 40
°C to 30 °C, and why the last few degrees take the longest.

## Heat sinks and fins
A heat sink is a block of metal, usually aluminium or copper, with many thin fins that draws heat away from a hot
component and passes it to the air. The fins greatly increase the surface area, so convection and radiation can carry
away more heat. A computer processor producing 100 W would overheat almost at once without a heat sink and fan.

## Heat exchangers
A heat exchanger transfers heat from one fluid to another without letting them mix, usually through thin metal walls.
Car radiators, the coils on the back of a refrigerator and the plate exchangers in building heating systems are all
heat exchangers. Running the two fluids in opposite directions, called counterflow, transfers the most heat because
the temperature difference stays large along the whole length.

## The vacuum flask
A vacuum flask keeps drinks hot or cold by blocking all three kinds of heat transfer. The vacuum between the double
walls of a vacuum flask stops conduction and convection, the silvered or polished surfaces reflect thermal radiation,
and the stopper limits heat loss through the opening. A good vacuum flask can keep coffee above 60 °C for 12 hours or
more.
