# Biotech basics — gene therapy and viral vectors

## What gene therapy is
Gene therapy treats disease by adding, replacing, silencing or editing genetic material in a patient's cells. The
most common approach delivers a working copy of a gene to make up for a faulty one, as in inherited diseases caused
by a single gene. Approved gene therapies change only the patient's body cells, called somatic cells, and not the
eggs or sperm that are passed to children.

## In vivo and ex vivo gene therapy
Gene therapy can be given in vivo, inside the body, or ex vivo, outside the body. In vivo gene therapy injects the
vector directly into the blood or into a target tissue such as the eye or the liver. Ex vivo gene therapy removes the
patient's cells, often blood stem cells or T cells, modifies them in the lab and then infuses them back into the
patient.

## What a viral vector is
A viral vector is a virus that has been engineered to deliver a therapeutic gene instead of causing disease. The
genes the virus needs to copy itself are removed and replaced with the therapeutic gene, so the vector can enter
cells once but cannot multiply. Viruses are used because they have evolved efficient ways to get through the cell
membrane and deliver genetic material to the nucleus.

## Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors
Adeno-associated virus (AAV) is the most widely used vector for in vivo gene therapy. AAV is a small virus, about 25
nm across, that is not known to cause disease in humans and mostly stays in the nucleus as a separate piece of DNA
instead of joining the chromosomes. The main limit of AAV is its small cargo of about 4.7 kb, and different versions
called serotypes, such as AAV2, AAV8 and AAV9, prefer different tissues.

## Lentiviral vectors
Lentiviral vectors are built from HIV-1 with all disease-causing genes removed, and they insert their genetic cargo
permanently into the host cell's chromosomes. Because the inserted gene is copied every time the cell divides,
lentiviral vectors suit ex vivo therapy of dividing cells such as blood stem cells and T cells. Lentiviral vectors
carry up to about 8 to 10 kb and are produced from several separate plasmids so that they cannot rebuild a working
virus.

## Adenoviral vectors
Adenoviral vectors are based on adenovirus, a cause of the common cold, and can carry genes of up to about 8 kb, or
more than 30 kb in helper-dependent versions. Adenoviral vectors enter many cell types and give strong but
short-lived expression, because the immune system reacts strongly against them. For this reason adenoviral vectors
are used more in vaccines and cancer therapy than for long-term gene replacement.

## CAR-T cell therapy
CAR-T cell therapy is an ex vivo gene therapy that reprograms a patient's own T cells to attack cancer. The T cells
are collected from the blood, given a gene for a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) using a lentiviral or
gammaretroviral vector, grown to large numbers and infused back. The first CAR-T therapies, approved in 2017, target
the CD19 protein on B-cell leukaemias and lymphomas.

## Producing viral vectors
Viral vectors are usually produced in HEK293 cells that are transfected with several plasmids at once. For AAV, one
plasmid carries the therapeutic gene, one carries the AAV rep and cap genes, and one carries helper genes from
adenovirus. After a few days the vector particles are harvested and purified, for example by affinity and
ion-exchange chromatography, and the dose is counted in vector genomes (vg), often 10¹³ to 10¹⁴ vg per kilogram for
AAV given into the blood.

## Immune responses and safety
Immune responses are a major safety concern in gene therapy with viral vectors. Many people already carry antibodies
against common AAV serotypes from natural infection, which can block the vector, and patients usually cannot receive
a second dose because they make antibodies after the first. Very high doses can damage the liver, and vectors that
integrate into the chromosomes carry a small risk of switching on a cancer gene near the insertion site.

## Approved gene therapies
Several gene therapies are now approved medicines for serious inherited diseases and cancers. Luxturna, approved in
the United States in 2017, uses AAV2 to deliver the RPE65 gene to the retina for an inherited form of blindness, and
Zolgensma, approved in 2019, uses AAV9 to deliver the SMN1 gene to infants with spinal muscular atrophy. In 2023
Casgevy became the first approved therapy based on CRISPR gene editing, for sickle cell disease.
