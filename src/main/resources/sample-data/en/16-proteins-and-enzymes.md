# Biotech basics — proteins and enzymes

## What a protein is
A protein is a chain of amino acids folded into a specific three-dimensional shape. Cells use 20 standard amino
acids, joined end to end by peptide bonds, and a typical protein has a few hundred of them. The order of amino acids,
set by a gene, decides how the chain folds and therefore what job the protein can do.

## The four levels of protein structure
Protein structure is described at four levels. The primary structure is the amino acid sequence; the secondary
structure is local folding into alpha helices and beta sheets; the tertiary structure is the full 3D fold of one
chain. The quaternary structure is how several chains fit together, as in haemoglobin, which has four.

## What an enzyme does
An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up. It works by lowering the activation
energy, the energy barrier the reaction must climb. Many enzymes make reactions millions of times faster, and one
enzyme molecule can process thousands of substrate molecules every second.

## The active site and substrate
The active site is the small pocket on an enzyme where the substrate binds and the reaction happens. Its shape and
chemistry fit only certain substrates, which is why each enzyme is specific. In the induced-fit model the active site
changes shape slightly as the substrate binds, gripping it more tightly.

## Temperature and enzyme activity
Enzyme activity rises with temperature up to an optimum and then falls sharply. Most human enzymes work best near
37 °C; above roughly 45–50 °C they start to unfold, or denature, and lose their shape. Enzymes from microbes that live
in hot springs can stay active above 80 °C.

## pH and enzyme activity
Each enzyme has an optimum pH at which it works fastest. Pepsin, which digests protein in the stomach, works best
near pH 2, while trypsin in the small intestine prefers about pH 8. Moving far from the optimum changes the charges on
the active site and can denature the enzyme.

## Enzyme kinetics: Vmax and Km
Vmax is the top speed an enzyme reaches when all its active sites are full of substrate. Km, the Michaelis constant,
is the substrate concentration that gives half of Vmax. A low Km means the enzyme binds its substrate tightly and
works well even when little substrate is present.

## Enzyme inhibitors
An enzyme inhibitor is a molecule that slows or stops an enzyme. A competitive inhibitor looks like the substrate and
blocks the active site, so adding more substrate can overcome it. A non-competitive inhibitor binds elsewhere on the
enzyme and changes its shape, so extra substrate does not help.

## Cofactors and coenzymes
A cofactor is a non-protein helper that some enzymes need to work. Cofactors can be metal ions such as zinc,
magnesium or iron, or small organic molecules called coenzymes, many of which are made from vitamins. NAD+, for
example, is a coenzyme made from vitamin B3 that carries electrons in energy reactions.

## Measuring protein concentration
Protein concentration is often measured by light absorbance. Proteins absorb ultraviolet light at 280 nm because of
the amino acids tryptophan and tyrosine, so a spectrophotometer can estimate the amount directly. Colour-based tests
such as the Bradford assay, read at 595 nm, are more sensitive and compare samples against a standard curve.
