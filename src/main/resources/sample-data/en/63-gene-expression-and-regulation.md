# Biotech basics — gene expression and regulation

## What gene expression is
Gene expression is the process by which the information in a gene is used to make a working product, usually a
protein. Gene expression includes transcription of DNA into RNA and translation of RNA into protein, and a cell can
control it at each of these steps. Almost every cell in the human body has the same DNA, so the differences between
a liver cell and a nerve cell come from which genes are expressed.

## Why cells regulate genes
Cells regulate gene expression so that each protein is made only where and when it is needed. A human cell
expresses only part of its roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes at any one time, often around half. Gene regulation
saves energy and materials, lets cells respond to signals such as hormones, nutrients or stress, and drives
development from a single fertilised egg into hundreds of cell types.

## Housekeeping and tissue-specific genes
Housekeeping genes are genes expressed in almost all cells at fairly steady levels because they handle basic tasks
such as energy production. Examples include GAPDH and β-actin, which are often used as reference genes in
laboratory experiments. Tissue-specific genes, by contrast, are switched on only in certain cells, such as insulin
in the beta cells of the pancreas or haemoglobin in developing red blood cells.

## Transcription factors
A transcription factor is a protein that binds a specific DNA sequence and turns transcription of nearby genes up
or down. Activators help RNA polymerase bind and start at the promoter, while repressors block it. Humans have
about 1,600 transcription factors, and most genes are controlled by a combination of several of them, which allows
very precise control.

## Enhancers and silencers
An enhancer is a DNA region that boosts transcription of a gene when activator proteins bind to it, and a silencer
is a region that reduces transcription. Enhancers can work from thousands or even a million base pairs away from
the gene, because the DNA loops around so that the enhancer touches the promoter. A single gene may have several
enhancers, each active in a different tissue.

## The lac operon in bacteria
The lac operon is a classic example of gene regulation in the bacterium Escherichia coli. Three genes for using the
sugar lactose share one promoter and are kept off by a repressor protein that binds a site called the operator.
When lactose is present, a form of it called allolactose binds the repressor and pulls it off the DNA, so the genes
are transcribed; when glucose is also present, expression stays low.

## Epigenetics: DNA methylation and histones
Epigenetic regulation changes gene expression without changing the DNA sequence. DNA methylation adds a methyl
group to cytosines, mostly at CpG sites, and genes with heavily methylated promoters are usually silent. DNA is
also wrapped around histone proteins, and chemical tags on histones such as acetylation loosen this packing and
make genes easier to read, while some histone methylation marks tighten it.

## Regulation after transcription
Gene expression is also controlled after an mRNA has been made. Alternative splicing, export of the mRNA from the
nucleus, how fast the mRNA is degraded and how often it is translated all change how much protein a gene yields.
MicroRNAs, about 22 nucleotides long, bind matching sequences in target mRNAs and reduce their translation or speed
up their breakdown.

## Regulation of proteins
Gene expression can also be tuned at the level of the finished protein. Cells add or remove phosphate groups to
switch enzymes on or off within seconds, and they tag unwanted proteins with ubiquitin so that the proteasome
destroys them. Protein half-lives range from minutes to many days, so protein breakdown is as important as protein
synthesis in setting protein levels.

## Measuring gene expression
Gene expression is usually measured by counting RNA or protein molecules. RT-qPCR measures the mRNA of a few chosen
genes very precisely, while RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) measures the expression of all genes at once. Protein levels
are checked with methods such as Western blot or ELISA, and reporter genes such as green fluorescent protein (GFP)
let researchers watch gene expression in living cells.
