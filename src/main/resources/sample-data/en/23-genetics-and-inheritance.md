# Biotech basics — genetics and inheritance

## Genes, alleles and traits
An allele is one version of a gene, and different alleles can give different traits. Most organisms that reproduce
sexually carry two copies of each gene, one from each parent. If the two alleles are the same the organism is
homozygous for that gene; if they differ it is heterozygous.

## Dominant and recessive alleles
A dominant allele shows its trait even when only one copy is present. A recessive allele shows its trait only when
both copies are recessive. In Mendel's pea plants, the purple-flower allele was dominant over the white-flower allele,
so only plants with two white alleles had white flowers.

## Genotype and phenotype
The genotype is the set of alleles an organism carries, while the phenotype is the trait you can observe. Two plants
with genotypes PP and Pp can both have purple flowers, so the same phenotype can hide different genotypes. The
environment can also shape the phenotype, for example height through nutrition.

## The Punnett square
A Punnett square is a grid that predicts the possible offspring of two parents. Each parent's alleles go along one
side, and the boxes show every combination. Crossing two heterozygous parents (Aa × Aa) gives on average 1 AA, 2 Aa
and 1 aa, a 3:1 ratio of dominant to recessive traits.

## Chromosomes
A chromosome is a long DNA molecule packed with proteins that carries many genes. Humans have 46 chromosomes in 23
pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes and one pair of sex chromosomes. Females usually have two X chromosomes and males have an
X and a Y.

## Mitosis
Mitosis is cell division that makes two daughter cells genetically identical to the parent cell. Each chromosome is
copied first, and the copies are then pulled to opposite ends of the cell before it splits. Mitosis is used for
growth, repair and replacing old cells, and each human daughter cell keeps all 46 chromosomes.

## Meiosis
Meiosis is cell division that makes sex cells, eggs and sperm, with half the normal number of chromosomes. It
involves two divisions and produces four cells, each with 23 chromosomes in humans. During meiosis, matching
chromosomes swap pieces in a process called crossing over, which mixes alleles and creates genetic variety.

## Sex-linked inheritance
Sex-linked traits are controlled by genes on the X chromosome. Because males have only one X, a single recessive
allele on it shows its trait, while females usually need two copies. This is why red-green colour blindness and
haemophilia are much more common in males than in females.

## Incomplete dominance and codominance
Some alleles are neither fully dominant nor recessive. In incomplete dominance the heterozygote shows a blend, as when
red and white snapdragons give pink flowers. In codominance both alleles show fully, as in human AB blood type, where
both A and B markers appear on red blood cells.

## Polygenic traits
A polygenic trait is controlled by many genes, each with a small effect. Human height, skin colour and eye colour
are examples. Because many genes add together, and the environment also plays a part, polygenic traits show a smooth
range of values that often forms a bell-shaped curve in a population.
