# Biotech basics — gel electrophoresis

## What gel electrophoresis does
Gel electrophoresis separates DNA, RNA or protein molecules by size using an electric field. The samples are loaded
into wells in a gel, and when the current is switched on the molecules travel through the gel. Smaller molecules move
faster and further, so after the run the molecules are spread out by size.

## Why DNA moves toward the positive electrode
DNA moves toward the positive electrode because its sugar-phosphate backbone carries a negative charge. The charge
is spread evenly along the molecule, so every DNA fragment has about the same charge per unit length. This means size,
not charge, decides how fast a fragment moves, which is the basis of the method.

## Agarose gels
Agarose gels are the standard choice for separating DNA fragments from about 100 to 25,000 base pairs. Agarose is a
sugar polymer from seaweed that is melted in buffer, poured into a tray and left to set. A 1% gel suits fragments of
about 0.5–10 kb, while a 2% gel gives better separation of small fragments.

## Running buffer: TAE and TBE
The running buffer carries the current and keeps the pH steady during electrophoresis. The two common buffers are
TAE (Tris-acetate-EDTA) and TBE (Tris-borate-EDTA). TAE is better for large fragments and recovering DNA from the gel,
while TBE gives sharper bands for small fragments and holds up better in long runs.

## Loading dye
Loading dye is mixed with each sample before it goes into the well. It contains glycerol or a similar dense agent
that makes the sample sink into the well instead of floating away. It also contains coloured tracking dyes, such as
bromophenol blue, that move ahead of the DNA so you can see how far the run has gone.

## The DNA ladder
A DNA ladder is a mixture of DNA fragments of known sizes run in one lane next to the samples. After staining it
looks like a set of rungs, each labelled with its size in base pairs. Comparing a sample band to the ladder gives an
estimate of that fragment's size.

## Voltage and run time
Agarose gels are usually run at about 5–8 volts per centimetre of distance between the electrodes. For a typical
small gel this means 80–120 V for 30–60 minutes. Running at too high a voltage heats the gel and gives smeared,
distorted bands.

## Staining and seeing the bands
DNA in a gel is invisible until it is stained with a dye that binds DNA and glows under light. Ethidium bromide
glows orange under UV light at about 300 nm, but it is a mutagen and must be handled with gloves. Safer dyes that glow
under blue light are now widely used instead.

## SDS-PAGE for proteins
SDS-PAGE separates proteins by size in a polyacrylamide gel. The detergent SDS unfolds the proteins and coats them
with negative charge, so they all move toward the positive electrode at a rate set by their mass. Bands are compared
to a protein ladder measured in kilodaltons (kDa) and are often stained with Coomassie blue.

## Reading a gel
A clean gel shows sharp, single bands at the expected size in each sample lane. A smear down the lane usually means
the DNA was degraded or the lane was overloaded. Extra bands can mean contamination or unwanted products, and an
empty lane suggests the sample failed or was not loaded.
