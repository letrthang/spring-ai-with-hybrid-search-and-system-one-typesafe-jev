# Physics basics — rotational motion

## Angular velocity
Angular velocity is how fast an object turns, measured in radians per second (rad/s). One full turn is 2π radians, so
ω = 2π f, where f is the number of turns per second. A wheel spinning at 600 revolutions per minute, or 10 turns per
second, has an angular velocity of about 63 rad/s. The linear speed at its rim is v = ω r.

## Centripetal force
An object moving in a circle needs a centripetal force pointing towards the centre, F = m v² / r. Without it, the
object would carry on in a straight line. For a 1,000 kg car taking a bend of radius 50 m at 10 m/s, friction from the
tyres must supply 2,000 N towards the centre of the bend.

## Centripetal acceleration
An object moving in a circle at constant speed is still accelerating, because its direction keeps changing. This
centripetal acceleration points towards the centre and equals a = v² / r = ω² r. A point on the rim of a 0.3 m radius
wheel turning at 20 rad/s has an acceleration of 120 m/s², more than 12 times g.

## Torque
Torque is the turning effect of a force, τ = F · r, the force times its perpendicular distance from the pivot,
measured in newton-metres (N·m). Pushing a door with 20 N at 0.8 m from the hinges gives 16 N·m of torque. That is why
handles are placed far from the hinges, and why a long spanner loosens a tight bolt more easily.

## The principle of moments
An object is balanced when the total clockwise moment about a pivot equals the total anticlockwise moment. On a
seesaw, a 30 kg child sitting 2 m from the middle balances a 60 kg adult sitting 1 m from it, because 30 × 2 = 60 × 1.
Tower cranes use counterweights on the same principle to avoid tipping over.

## Centre of mass and stability
The centre of mass is the point where an object's whole weight can be treated as acting. An object topples when its
centre of mass moves outside its base. Objects with a low centre of mass and a wide base, such as racing cars and
traffic cones, are very stable and hard to tip over.

## Moment of inertia
The moment of inertia measures how hard it is to change an object's rotation, the rotational version of mass. It
depends on both the mass and how far that mass is from the axis, I = Σ m r². A hoop is harder to spin up than a solid
disc of the same mass and radius, because all of its mass is at the rim.

## Angular momentum
Angular momentum is L = I · ω, and it stays constant if no outside torque acts. A spinning ice skater who pulls in her
arms reduces her moment of inertia, so she spins faster. Pulling the arms in far enough to halve the moment of inertia
doubles the rate of spin.

## Rotational kinetic energy
A spinning object has rotational kinetic energy, E = ½ I ω². Flywheels store energy this way: a heavy wheel spinning
fast can hold thousands of joules and release them smoothly. A ball rolling down a slope shares its energy between
moving forward and spinning, so it reaches the bottom more slowly than a block sliding without friction.

## Gyroscopes
A spinning gyroscope resists changes to the direction of its axis, because turning the axis needs a torque to change
its angular momentum. That is why a spinning top stays upright and a spinning frisbee or rifle bullet keeps a steady
orientation in flight. Gyroscopes help steady ships, aircraft instruments and the cameras on drones.
