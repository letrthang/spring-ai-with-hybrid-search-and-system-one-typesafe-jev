# RAS basics — Water sources and intake treatment

## Common water sources for a RAS
A RAS usually takes its water from a borehole, a municipal mains supply, a river or lake, or the sea for marine
species. Borehole water is often preferred because it is stable in temperature and normally free of fish pathogens.
Surface water from rivers, lakes or the sea is easier and cheaper to reach but carries more disease risk, so it
needs stronger treatment before use.

## How much intake water a RAS needs
A RAS needs only a small volume of new water, typically 1–10% of the system volume per day or about 100–1000 L per
kilogram of feed. Because the demand is small, a farm can afford to treat every litre of intake water carefully.
The intake must still be sized for the peak demand when tanks are refilled after a harvest or cleaning.

## Testing a new water source
A new water source should be tested over several seasons before a RAS is built, because quality can change between
wet and dry months. A full test covers pH, alkalinity, hardness, iron, manganese, nitrate, ammonia, hydrogen
sulphide, dissolved gases, heavy metals and pesticides. Testing early avoids building a farm on water that later
proves unsuitable.

## Iron and manganese in groundwater
Borehole water often contains dissolved iron and manganese, which oxidise into brown or black deposits that clog
filters, foul sensors and irritate fish gills. Iron above about 0.1 mg/L is usually removed by aerating the water
and passing it through a sand filter or an oxidising media filter. Manganese is harder to remove and may need a
higher pH or a stronger oxidant.

## Degassing borehole water
Borehole water is often low in oxygen and supersaturated with carbon dioxide or nitrogen, and it may contain
hydrogen sulphide. A packed column or cascade aerator at the intake strips these gases and adds oxygen before the
water reaches the fish. Untreated supersaturated water can cause gas bubble disease, with visible bubbles in the
fins and eyes.

## Removing chlorine from mains water
Municipal mains water contains chlorine or chloramine, which are toxic to fish and to biofilter bacteria even at
very low levels, and safe limits for fish are usually given as below 0.01 mg/L. Activated carbon filters remove
both chlorine and chloramine, and sodium thiosulphate can neutralise chlorine in an emergency. Mains water should
be checked with a chlorine test kit before each large refill.

## Filtering and disinfecting surface water
Surface water from a river, lake or the sea is first screened and sand-filtered to remove particles, then
disinfected to kill pathogens and parasites. Many farms filter to 10–20 µm and follow with a high-dose UV unit or
ozone. The intake disinfection step is one of the most important biosecurity barriers in a RAS.

## Storing and conditioning make-up water
Many farms store treated intake water in a covered reservoir tank before it enters the RAS. The reservoir lets the
water settle, reach the system temperature and be adjusted for pH, alkalinity or salinity. A reservoir holding at
least one day of make-up water also protects the farm if the source is interrupted.

## Hard and soft water
Water hardness is the amount of calcium and magnesium in the water, and alkalinity is the water's ability to buffer
pH. Very soft water with alkalinity below about 50 mg/L as CaCO3 needs extra bicarbonate to support nitrification.
Very hard water can leave scale on heat exchangers, pipes and sensors.

## Monitoring intake water quality
Intake water quality should be checked regularly even after the farm is running, because sources can change with
season, rainfall or nearby activity. Many farms test intake pH, temperature and salinity daily and send samples for
a full laboratory analysis every few months. A sudden change in the intake is a common hidden cause of fish stress
in a RAS.
