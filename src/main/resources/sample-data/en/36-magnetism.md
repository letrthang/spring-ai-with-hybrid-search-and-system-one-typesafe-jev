# Physics basics — magnetism

## Magnetic poles
Every magnet has a north pole and a south pole; like poles repel and unlike poles attract. Cutting a bar magnet in
half does not separate the poles, because each piece becomes a new magnet with its own north and south. The north pole
of a compass needle is the end that points towards Earth's geographic north.

## Magnetic field lines
A magnetic field is the region around a magnet where magnetic forces act, drawn as field lines running from north to
south outside the magnet. Lines packed closely together show a strong field, so the field is strongest near the poles.
Iron filings sprinkled around a magnet line up along these field lines.

## Earth's magnetic field
Earth behaves like a giant bar magnet, with a field produced by electric currents in its molten iron outer core. The
field at the surface is about 25 to 65 microtesla. The magnetic pole in the far north is actually a south magnetic
pole, which is why it attracts the north end of a compass needle.

## Magnetic materials
Iron, nickel and cobalt are ferromagnetic: they are strongly attracted to magnets and can be magnetised. Soft iron
magnetises and demagnetises easily, so it is used in electromagnets and transformer cores, while steel and neodymium
alloys keep their magnetism and make permanent magnets. Heating a magnet above its Curie temperature, 770 °C for iron,
destroys its magnetism.

## The magnetic field of a current
An electric current produces a magnetic field that circles around the wire. The right-hand grip rule gives its
direction: point your right thumb along the current and your fingers curl the way the field goes. The field gets
stronger with more current and weaker farther from the wire.

## Electromagnets
An electromagnet is a coil of wire, usually wound around a soft iron core, that becomes a magnet only while current
flows. Its strength increases with more current, more turns of wire and an iron core. Electromagnets can be switched
on and off, which is why they are used in scrapyard cranes, door locks, relays and MRI scanners.

## The motor effect
A wire carrying a current in a magnetic field feels a force, F = B · I · L, where B is the field strength in tesla, I
the current and L the length of wire in the field. A 0.5 m wire carrying 4 A at right angles to a 0.2 T field feels a
force of 0.4 N. Fleming's left-hand rule gives the direction of the force.

## Electric motors
An electric motor turns electrical energy into rotation using the force on current-carrying coils in a magnetic field.
The forces on the two sides of the coil point in opposite directions, making it spin. A commutator or an electronic
controller reverses the current every half turn so the coil keeps turning the same way.

## Electromagnetic induction
Moving a magnet near a coil, or changing the magnetic field through it, induces a voltage in the coil (Faraday's law).
The induced voltage is larger when the change is faster, the field stronger or the coil has more turns. Lenz's law
says the induced current always opposes the change that caused it. Generators make electricity this way by spinning
coils in magnetic fields.

## Transformers
A transformer changes the voltage of an alternating current using two coils wound on a shared iron core. The voltage
ratio equals the turns ratio, Vs / Vp = Ns / Np, so a secondary coil with 20 times more turns steps 230 V up to 4,600
V. Transformers only work with AC, because induction needs a changing magnetic field.
