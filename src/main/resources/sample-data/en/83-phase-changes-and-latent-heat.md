# Physics basics — phase changes and latent heat

## The six phase changes
A phase change is a substance switching between solid, liquid and gas without changing what the substance is made of.
Melting turns a solid into a liquid and freezing does the reverse; vaporisation turns a liquid into a gas and
condensation does the reverse. Sublimation takes a solid straight to a gas, and deposition takes a gas straight to a
solid.

## Why temperature stays constant during a phase change
During a phase change the temperature of a substance stays constant even though heat keeps flowing in or out. The
added energy goes into breaking or loosening the bonds between particles rather than making the particles move faster.
A pan of water on a stove therefore stays at 100 °C while it boils, however high the flame, as long as the pressure
stays at 1 atm.

## Specific latent heat
Specific latent heat L is the energy needed to change the phase of 1 kg of a substance at constant temperature, and
the heat required is Q = m · L. Melting 0.2 kg of ice needs Q = 0.2 kg × 334 kJ/kg = 66.8 kJ. The same amount of
energy is released when the substance changes back, so freezing 0.2 kg of water gives out 66.8 kJ.

## Fusion versus vaporisation
For water, the specific latent heat of vaporisation (about 2,260 kJ/kg) is almost seven times the specific latent heat
of fusion (about 334 kJ/kg). Melting only loosens the bonds so that molecules can slide past one another, while
boiling must pull the molecules completely apart and push back the surrounding air. That is why steam at 100 °C causes
far worse burns than water at 100 °C.

## Evaporation versus boiling
Evaporation is vaporisation that happens only at the surface of a liquid and can occur at any temperature, as when a
puddle dries on a cool day. Boiling happens throughout the liquid, forming bubbles of vapour, and only at the boiling
point, where the vapour pressure equals the surrounding pressure. Evaporation is faster when the liquid is warmer, the
surface area larger, the air drier and the wind stronger.

## Boiling point and pressure
The boiling point of a liquid depends on the pressure above it: lower pressure lowers the boiling point and higher
pressure raises it. On the summit of Mount Everest, at about a third of sea-level pressure, water boils at about 70
°C, so food cooks slowly. A pressure cooker raises the pressure to about 2 atm, so water boils at about 120 °C and
food cooks much faster.

## Evaporative cooling
Evaporative cooling happens because the fastest-moving molecules escape from the surface of a liquid, taking latent
heat with them and leaving the remaining liquid cooler. Sweat cools the body this way: evaporating 1 litre of sweat
removes about 2,400 kJ of heat. Evaporative cooling works poorly in humid air, which is why hot, humid days feel more
uncomfortable than hot, dry ones.

## Condensation and the dew point
Condensation is water vapour turning back into liquid when air cools to its dew point, the temperature at which the
air becomes saturated. Dew on grass, mist on a bathroom mirror and droplets on a cold drink all form this way.
Condensing water vapour releases its latent heat, about 2,260 kJ per kilogram, and this released heat powers the
strong updraughts inside thunderstorms.

## Sublimation and deposition
Sublimation is a solid turning directly into a gas without melting first. Dry ice, which is solid carbon dioxide,
sublimes at −78.5 °C at normal pressure, and freeze-drying uses sublimation to remove ice from frozen food under
vacuum. Deposition is the reverse change, gas straight to solid, and deposition is how frost forms on windows and car
roofs on clear, cold nights.

## Heating ice to steam
Turning 1 kg of ice at −20 °C into steam at 100 °C takes about 3,060 kJ in four stages. Warming the ice to 0 °C takes
about 42 kJ (the specific heat of ice is about 2,100 J/(kg·K)), melting it takes 334 kJ, warming the water to 100 °C
takes about 420 kJ, and boiling it away takes about 2,260 kJ. A graph of temperature against heat added shows sloping
parts for warming and flat plateaus for melting and boiling.
