# Biotech basics — RNA and transcription

## What RNA is
RNA, or ribonucleic acid, is a single-stranded nucleic acid that carries and uses genetic information in the cell.
RNA differs from DNA in three ways: its sugar is ribose instead of deoxyribose, it uses the base uracil (U) in
place of thymine (T), and it usually exists as one strand rather than a double helix. Because of the extra hydroxyl
group on ribose, RNA is much less stable than DNA and is broken down within minutes to hours.

## The main types of RNA
The three main types of RNA used in making proteins are messenger RNA, transfer RNA and ribosomal RNA. Messenger
RNA (mRNA) carries the copy of a gene to the ribosome, transfer RNA (tRNA) brings the matching amino acids, and
ribosomal RNA (rRNA) forms the core of the ribosome itself. Cells also make many small regulatory RNAs, such as
microRNAs about 22 nucleotides long, that turn genes down.

## What transcription is
Transcription is the process in which a cell copies the sequence of one gene from DNA into RNA. Only one of the two
DNA strands, the template strand, is read, and the new RNA has the same sequence as the other strand, the coding
strand, except that U replaces T. Transcription is the first step of gene expression and happens in the nucleus in
eukaryotes and in the cytoplasm in bacteria.

## RNA polymerase
RNA polymerase is the enzyme that builds an RNA chain during transcription. RNA polymerase unwinds a short stretch
of DNA, about 12–14 base pairs, reads the template strand and joins ribonucleotides in the 5′ to 3′ direction
without needing a primer. In bacteria and human cells RNA polymerase adds roughly 20–80 nucleotides per second.
Bacteria have one RNA polymerase, while eukaryotes have three main ones, and RNA polymerase II makes mRNA.

## Promoters and the start of transcription
A promoter is the DNA sequence just upstream of a gene where RNA polymerase binds to start transcription. Many
eukaryotic promoters contain a TATA box, a short sequence rich in T and A about 25–30 base pairs before the start
site, which is recognised by helper proteins called general transcription factors. In bacteria, a sigma factor
guides RNA polymerase to promoter elements at positions −10 and −35.

## Elongation and termination
During elongation, RNA polymerase moves along the template strand and adds one nucleotide at a time to the growing
RNA chain. Behind the enzyme the DNA closes up again, so only a small bubble of DNA is open at any moment.
Termination ends transcription: in bacteria a hairpin loop in the RNA or the Rho protein releases the transcript,
while in eukaryotes the RNA is cut after a polyadenylation signal, AAUAAA.

## The 5′ cap and poly(A) tail
In eukaryotes, a new messenger RNA is protected by a 5′ cap and a poly(A) tail before it leaves the nucleus. The 5′
cap is a modified guanine, 7-methylguanosine, added to the front end of the RNA, and it helps the ribosome find the
mRNA. The poly(A) tail is a string of about 200 adenines added to the 3′ end, which slows decay and helps move the
mRNA out of the nucleus.

## Splicing: introns and exons
Splicing is the step that removes introns from a eukaryotic pre-mRNA and joins the exons together. Exons are the
parts kept in the mature mRNA, while introns are non-coding stretches cut out by a large RNA–protein machine called
the spliceosome. An average human gene has about 8–9 exons, and its introns are often much longer than its exons.

## Alternative splicing
Alternative splicing lets one gene produce several different mRNAs, and therefore several proteins, by joining its
exons in different combinations. About 95% of human genes that have more than one exon are alternatively spliced.
Alternative splicing helps explain how only about 20,000 human protein-coding genes can give rise to a much larger
variety of proteins in different tissues.

## mRNA lifetime
The lifetime of a messenger RNA sets how long a gene keeps producing protein after transcription stops. In
mammalian cells the median mRNA half-life is about 9 hours, but some mRNAs last only minutes and others for days,
while bacterial mRNAs usually last just a few minutes. Cells control mRNA decay by shortening the poly(A) tail,
removing the 5′ cap and cutting the RNA with nucleases.
