# Biotech basics — genomics and the microbiome

## What genomics is
Genomics is the study of an organism's whole genome, all of its DNA, rather than one gene at a time. The human
genome has about 3.1 billion base pairs and roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes, which make up only about 1–2% of
the DNA. Genomics asks how all these genes are arranged, how they are switched on and off, and how they differ
between individuals and species.

## The Human Genome Project
The Human Genome Project was an international effort to read the first complete human DNA sequence. The Human
Genome Project ran from 1990 to 2003 and cost about 3 billion US dollars. Thanks to faster sequencing machines, a
whole human genome can now be read in a day or two for a few hundred dollars, which has made genomics part of
everyday research and medicine.

## Genetic variation between people
Any two people share about 99.9% of their DNA sequence, yet the remaining 0.1% still adds up to millions of
differences. The most common kind is the single-nucleotide polymorphism, or SNP, where one base differs, and a
typical person carries about 4 to 5 million SNPs compared with the reference genome. Most variants are harmless,
but some affect traits, disease risk or how drugs work.

## Genome-wide association studies
A genome-wide association study, or GWAS, scans the genomes of many people to find variants that are more common in
people with a certain trait or disease. A GWAS often compares hundreds of thousands of people and tests millions of
SNPs at once. Because so many tests are run, a result usually needs a p-value below 5 × 10^-8 to count as
significant.

## Pharmacogenomics
Pharmacogenomics is the study of how a person's genes affect their response to medicines. Variants in liver enzymes
such as CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 make some people break down certain drugs very fast or very slowly, so the same dose can
be too weak or too strong. Doctors test for the HLA-B*57:01 variant before prescribing the HIV drug abacavir,
because carriers can have a severe allergic reaction.

## What the microbiome is
The microbiome is the whole community of bacteria, fungi, viruses and other microbes living in a particular place,
together with their genes. A human body hosts roughly 38 trillion bacteria, about the same number as its own human
cells, and most of them live in the large intestine. Soil, oceans, plant roots and fish tanks all have their own
microbiomes too.

## The gut microbiome
The gut microbiome is the community of microbes in the digestive tract, mainly the large intestine, where each
person carries a few hundred bacterial species. Gut bacteria ferment dietary fibre that human enzymes cannot
digest, producing short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate that feed the cells of the gut wall. Gut microbes also
make some vitamin K and help train the immune system.

## 16S rRNA sequencing
16S rRNA sequencing identifies which bacteria are in a sample by reading one marker gene instead of whole genomes.
The 16S rRNA gene, about 1,500 base pairs long, is found in all bacteria and has conserved regions mixed with
variable regions such as V4. PCR copies a variable region from every bacterium in the sample, and the sequences are
matched to databases to name the bacteria, usually down to genus level.

## Shotgun metagenomics
Shotgun metagenomics sequences all the DNA in a sample, from every organism present, instead of a single marker
gene. The millions of short reads are sorted by computer to identify species, including fungi and viruses that 16S
sequencing misses, and to list the genes the community carries, such as antibiotic-resistance genes. Shotgun
metagenomics gives more detail than 16S sequencing but costs more per sample.

## Changing the microbiome
The microbiome changes with diet, age, medicines and environment. A course of antibiotics can wipe out many gut
species for weeks, while a diet rich in fibre supports a more diverse community. Faecal microbiota transplant,
which moves gut microbes from a healthy donor to a patient, cures about 80–90% of recurrent Clostridioides
difficile infections that keep returning after antibiotics.
