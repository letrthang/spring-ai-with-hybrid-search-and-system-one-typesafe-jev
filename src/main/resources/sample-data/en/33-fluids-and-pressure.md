# Physics basics — fluids and pressure

## What pressure is
Pressure is force divided by the area it acts on, p = F / A, measured in pascals (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N/m². The same
force gives a higher pressure on a smaller area, which is why a sharp knife cuts easily and snowshoes stop you sinking
into snow. A 600 N person standing on 0.04 m² of shoe soles exerts 15,000 Pa.

## Pressure in liquids
Pressure in a liquid increases with depth: p = ρ g h, where ρ is the density of the liquid and h the depth. In water,
pressure rises by about one atmosphere (about 100 kPa) for every 10 m of depth. Pressure at a given depth acts equally
in all directions and does not depend on the shape of the container.

## Atmospheric pressure
Atmospheric pressure is the weight of the air above pressing down, about 101,325 Pa (101 kPa) at sea level. It falls
with altitude: at the top of Mount Everest it is only about a third of the sea-level value. Air pressure is measured
with a barometer, and weather forecasts often quote it in hectopascals, around 1,013 hPa.

## Gauge and absolute pressure
Absolute pressure is measured from a perfect vacuum, while gauge pressure is measured relative to the surrounding air.
A tyre gauge reading 220 kPa means the absolute pressure inside is about 320 kPa, because about 100 kPa of atmospheric
pressure must be added. Absolute pressure = gauge pressure + atmospheric pressure.

## Density
Density is mass per unit volume, ρ = m / V, measured in kilograms per cubic metre. Fresh water has a density of about
1,000 kg/m³, air about 1.2 kg/m³, aluminium 2,700 kg/m³ and steel about 7,800 kg/m³. An object less dense than a fluid
floats in it; a denser one sinks.

## Buoyancy and Archimedes' principle
A body in a fluid is pushed upward by a buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces (Archimedes'
principle). A 1-litre block fully under water displaces 1 kg of water, so it feels an upward force of about 9.8 N. A
floating ship sinks just deep enough to displace its own weight of water.

## Pascal's principle and hydraulics
Pressure applied to a trapped liquid is passed on equally to every part of it (Pascal's principle). In a hydraulic
jack, a small force on a narrow piston creates a pressure that pushes on a wide piston, multiplying the force. With
pistons of 1 cm² and 100 cm², a 50 N push lifts a 5,000 N load.

## Bernoulli's principle
Bernoulli's principle says that where a fluid flows faster, its pressure is lower. Air speeding over a curved surface
or through a narrowing produces a pressure drop, which helps explain the lift on aircraft wings and how a perfume
atomiser draws up liquid. It holds for steady flow and follows from conservation of energy.

## Flow rate and continuity
Flow rate is the volume of fluid passing a point each second, Q = A · v, where A is the cross-section area and v the
flow speed. For a liquid in a closed pipe the flow rate is the same everywhere, so where the pipe narrows the liquid
speeds up. Halving the area doubles the speed, which is why a thumb over a garden hose makes a jet.

## Viscosity
Viscosity is a fluid's resistance to flowing, a kind of internal friction. Honey has a viscosity thousands of times
that of water, so it pours slowly. Most liquids become less viscous when heated, which is why warm syrup runs freely,
while gases become slightly more viscous as they warm up. Engine oils are graded by how their viscosity changes with
temperature.
