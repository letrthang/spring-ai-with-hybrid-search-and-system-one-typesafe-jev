# RAS basics — Starting up and cycling a new system

## What cycling a new RAS means
Cycling a new RAS means growing a population of nitrifying bacteria in the biofilter before fish are stocked.
Nitrifying bacteria convert toxic ammonia to nitrite and then to nitrate, and a new system has almost none of them.
Without cycling, ammonia and nitrite rise quickly after the first fish are stocked and can kill them.

## Commissioning before cycling
Before cycling starts, a new RAS should be filled, run with clean water and checked for leaks, correct flows and
working sensors and alarms. Pumps, drum filters, degassers, oxygen systems and UV units should all be tested, and
the backup generator should be run under load. Fixing faults before the system holds fish or bacteria is much
easier and safer.

## Fishless cycling with an ammonia source
Fishless cycling feeds the biofilter with an ammonia source, such as ammonium chloride or a small amount of fish
feed, instead of using live fish. A common approach is to dose TAN to about 2–5 mg/L and to dose again each time it
falls close to zero. Fishless cycling avoids exposing fish to high ammonia and nitrite while the bacteria grow.

## Seeding the biofilter
Seeding means adding nitrifying bacteria to speed up biofilter start-up, either from a commercial bacteria culture
or from mature media taken from a healthy RAS. Media from another farm can carry diseases, so it should only come
from a source with a known good health status. Seeding can shorten cycling from about 6–8 weeks to 2–4 weeks.

## Water conditions during cycling
Nitrifying bacteria grow fastest at a stable temperature close to the planned operating temperature, a pH of about
7.0–8.0, alkalinity above about 100 mg/L as CaCO3 and plenty of dissolved oxygen. UV and ozone are often turned off
or reduced during cycling so they do not slow bacterial growth. Low temperature or low pH can make cycling take
twice as long.

## The ammonia and nitrite peaks
During cycling, TAN rises first and then falls as ammonia-oxidising bacteria grow, after which nitrite rises and
falls as nitrite-oxidising bacteria catch up. The nitrite peak usually comes later and lasts longer than the
ammonia peak. A slow increase in nitrate is a sign that the full nitrification process is working.

## Testing during cycling
During cycling, TAN, nitrite, pH and alkalinity should be measured every day or every second day with a photometer
or test kit. Results should be plotted so the ammonia and nitrite peaks are easy to follow. Alkalinity must be
topped up during cycling because nitrification consumes about 7 g of alkalinity for every gram of TAN converted.

## When a new RAS is ready for fish
A new RAS is usually considered ready for fish when the biofilter can convert a full ammonia dose to nitrate within
24 hours, with TAN and nitrite both below about 0.5 mg/L. The biofilter should show this result for several days in
a row. A ready biofilter still needs time to grow to full capacity for the planned feed load.

## Stocking a new system gradually
A newly cycled RAS should be stocked and fed gradually, because the biofilter's capacity matches the bacteria grown
so far, not yet the full planned feed load. Many farms start at 25–50% of the planned feed load and increase it
over several weeks while checking TAN and nitrite daily. Increasing feed too fast is the most common cause of
ammonia spikes in a new RAS.

## Common start-up mistakes
Common mistakes when starting a new RAS are stocking fish before cycling is complete, letting alkalinity fall so
nitrification stalls and using chlorinated water that kills the bacteria. Another mistake is cleaning new biofilter
media too hard, which removes the young bacterial film. Patience in the first two months saves much larger losses
later.
