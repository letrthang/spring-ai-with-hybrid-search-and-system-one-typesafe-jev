# Physics basics — thermodynamics

## Thermal equilibrium and the zeroth law
Two objects in thermal contact exchange heat until they reach the same temperature, a state called thermal
equilibrium. The zeroth law of thermodynamics says that if A and B are each in equilibrium with C, they are in
equilibrium with each other. This is what makes a thermometer work: it settles to the temperature of whatever it
touches.

## Absolute zero and the kelvin scale
Absolute zero, 0 K or −273.15 °C, is the lowest possible temperature, where particles have the least possible energy.
The kelvin scale starts there and uses steps the same size as degrees Celsius, so K = °C + 273.15. Water freezes at
273.15 K and boils at 373.15 K at sea-level pressure.

## The first law of thermodynamics
The first law of thermodynamics says the change in a system's internal energy equals the heat added to it minus the
work it does: ΔU = Q − W. If a gas absorbs 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work pushing a piston, its internal energy
rises by 300 J. It is conservation of energy applied to heat.

## The second law of thermodynamics
The second law of thermodynamics says heat flows naturally from hot to cold, never the other way on its own. Put
another way, the entropy of an isolated system, a measure of how spread out its energy is, never decreases. That is
why a cup of coffee always cools to room temperature and never heats itself up by drawing heat from the room.

## Heat engines and efficiency
A heat engine turns some of the heat flowing from a hot source to a cold sink into work. Its efficiency is useful work
out divided by heat in, and it can never reach 100%. The best possible (Carnot) efficiency is 1 − T_cold / T_hot in
kelvin, so an engine working between 600 K and 300 K can convert at most 50%.

## Refrigerators and heat pumps
A refrigerator or heat pump uses work to move heat from a cold place to a warm one, against its natural direction. A
fluid called a refrigerant evaporates inside the cold space, absorbing heat, and condenses outside, releasing it. A
home heat pump can deliver 3 to 4 kWh of heat for every 1 kWh of electricity it uses.

## Latent heat
Latent heat is the energy needed to change a substance's state without changing its temperature. Melting 1 kg of ice
at 0 °C takes about 334 kJ, and boiling 1 kg of water at 100 °C takes about 2,260 kJ. While ice melts in a drink, the
temperature stays at 0 °C until all the ice is gone.

## Conduction
Conduction is heat passing through a material from particle to particle, without the material itself moving. Metals
are good conductors because free electrons carry energy quickly; copper conducts heat more than 10,000 times better
than air. Poor conductors such as wool, foam and trapped air are called insulators and keep houses and bodies warm.

## Convection
Convection is heat carried by the movement of a fluid. Warm fluid expands, becomes less dense and rises, while cooler,
denser fluid sinks to take its place, setting up a convection current. This is how a radiator heats a room, how water
in a pan heats evenly, and how sea breezes form on sunny coasts.

## Thermal radiation
Thermal radiation is heat carried by infrared electromagnetic waves, so it can cross empty space, which is how
sunlight warms Earth. All objects radiate, and hotter ones radiate far more: the power rises with the fourth power of
absolute temperature. Dark, matt surfaces absorb and emit radiation well, while shiny surfaces reflect it, which is
why emergency blankets are silver.
