# Physics basics — waves and sound

## Transverse and longitudinal waves
In a transverse wave the particles vibrate at right angles to the direction the wave travels, as on a shaken rope or
in light. In a longitudinal wave they vibrate back and forth along the direction of travel, as in sound. Both kinds
carry energy from place to place without carrying the material along with them.

## Amplitude, wavelength and frequency
Amplitude is the maximum displacement of a wave from its rest position, wavelength is the distance between two
neighbouring crests, and frequency is the number of waves passing a point each second, measured in hertz (Hz). The
period is the time for one wave, T = 1 / f, so a 50 Hz wave has a period of 0.02 s.

## Wavelength of a sound
The wavelength of a sound is its speed divided by its frequency, λ = v / f. In air at 20 °C, where sound travels at
343 m/s, a 343 Hz tone has a wavelength of 1 m and concert pitch A at 440 Hz about 78 cm. Low bass notes have
wavelengths of several metres, while very high notes are only a few centimetres long.

## Speed of sound in different materials
Sound travels at about 343 m/s in air at 20 °C, about 1,480 m/s in water and around 5,900 m/s in steel. It moves
faster in stiffer materials and in warmer air, gaining about 0.6 m/s for every degree Celsius. Sound cannot travel
through a vacuum, because it needs particles to pass the vibration along.

## Pitch and loudness
Pitch is how high or low a sound seems and depends on its frequency; loudness depends on its amplitude. Doubling the
frequency raises a note by one octave, so 220 Hz, 440 Hz and 880 Hz are all the note A. A bigger vibration pushes the
air harder and carries more energy, so the sound is louder.

## Decibels
Sound level is measured in decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale on which every 10 dB increase means ten times more sound
intensity. A quiet library is about 40 dB, normal conversation about 60 dB and a rock concert about 110 dB. Long
exposure to sound above about 85 dB can permanently damage hearing.

## Range of human hearing
Healthy young people hear frequencies from about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Sound above 20 kHz is called ultrasound, used in
medical scans and by bats, and sound below 20 Hz is infrasound, produced by elephants and earthquakes. The upper limit
falls with age, often to below 15 kHz by middle age.

## Echoes
An echo is sound reflected from a hard surface back to the listener. Because sound travels at about 343 m/s, a clap
that returns from a cliff after 2 seconds means the cliff is about 343 m away, since the sound went there and back.
Sonar and ultrasound scanners measure distances using the same echo timing.

## The Doppler effect
The Doppler effect is the change in pitch heard when a sound source moves relative to the listener. As an ambulance
approaches, its sound waves are squashed together, so the siren sounds higher; as it moves away, they are stretched
out and the pitch drops. Police speed guns and weather radar use the same effect to measure speed.

## Resonance and standing waves
Resonance happens when something is pushed at its natural frequency, so its vibrations build up to a large amplitude.
A guitar string forms a standing wave with fixed points called nodes at both ends; its lowest note has a wavelength
twice the string's length. Tightening the string raises its natural frequency and therefore its pitch.
