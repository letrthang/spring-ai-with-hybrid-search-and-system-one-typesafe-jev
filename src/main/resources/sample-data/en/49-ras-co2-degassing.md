# RAS basics — Carbon dioxide and degassing

## Why carbon dioxide builds up in a RAS
Carbon dioxide builds up in a RAS because fish and bacteria breathe it out and the reused water has no natural way to
lose it. Pure oxygen makes the problem worse, because it adds oxygen without the air contact that would also strip
CO2. Without a degassing step, CO2 can reach harmful levels within hours.

## Safe CO2 levels for fish
Most RAS farms keep dissolved carbon dioxide below 15 to 20 mg/L in the fish tanks. Salmon and trout are sensitive and
are best kept below 10 to 15 mg/L, while tilapia tolerate somewhat more. High CO2 makes it harder for fish to release
CO2 from their blood, which reduces their oxygen uptake.

## Signs of high CO2 in fish
Fish exposed to high CO2 become sluggish, eat less, grow more slowly and may breathe faster even when oxygen is high.
Long-term exposure can cause kidney stones (nephrocalcinosis) in salmon and trout. Because these signs are vague, CO2
should be measured rather than guessed from fish behaviour.

## What a CO2 stripper does
A CO2 stripper, or degasser, removes carbon dioxide by bringing water into close contact with large amounts of fresh
air. Water trickles down through a column of plastic packing while a fan blows air upward through it. The CO2 moves
from the water into the air, which is then vented out of the building.

## Air-to-water ratio in a degasser
A cascade CO2 stripper usually needs an air-to-water volume ratio of about 5:1 to 10:1 to work well. Too little air
lets the exhaust air fill up with CO2 and stripping stops. The exhaust must go outside the building, or the CO2 will
simply return to the water through the room air.

## How CO2 affects pH
Carbon dioxide dissolves in water to form carbonic acid, so high CO2 lowers the pH. In a RAS with low alkalinity, a
rise in CO2 after feeding can drop pH by 0.3 to 0.5 units. Stripping out CO2 raises pH again without adding any
chemicals.

## Measuring dissolved CO2
Dissolved CO2 can be measured directly with an in-line CO2 sensor or estimated from pH, alkalinity and temperature
using a standard chart. Direct sensors are more accurate but need regular calibration and membrane changes. Many farms
check CO2 by hand once or twice a day if they do not have a sensor.

## Degassing nitrogen as well as CO2
A degasser also removes excess nitrogen gas, which can build up when pumps suck in air or when cold water warms up.
Nitrogen supersaturation causes gas bubble disease in fish. Keeping total gas pressure below about 100 to 102 % is
one of the jobs of the degassing stage.

## Where the degasser sits in the loop
The CO2 stripper is usually placed after the biofilter and before the oxygenation step. The biofilter produces CO2,
so stripping it after the biofilter removes the most gas. Adding oxygen after degassing avoids blowing away the
expensive pure oxygen in the stripper.

## Common degasser faults
The most common degasser faults are a stopped fan, blocked packing and a closed or blocked exhaust duct. Any of these
causes CO2 in the fish tanks to rise slowly over several hours. Monitoring fan status and checking the packing for
biofilm and scale each month prevent most problems.
