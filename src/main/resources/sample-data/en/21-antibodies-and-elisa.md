# Biotech basics — antibodies and ELISA

## What an antibody is
An antibody is a Y-shaped protein made by B cells of the immune system that binds tightly to one specific target.
The target is called an antigen, and the small part of it the antibody recognises is called an epitope. Because
antibodies bind so specifically, they are used as tools to detect and measure molecules in the lab.

## Antibody structure
An antibody is made of four protein chains: two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains joined by
disulfide bonds. The tips of the two arms form the variable region, which binds the antigen. The stem, called the Fc
region, is the same within a class and is recognised by other immune cells and by lab reagents.

## Antibody classes
Humans make five classes of antibody: IgG, IgM, IgA, IgE and IgD. IgG is the most common in blood, at about 75% of
the total, and is the main antibody used in lab tests. IgM appears first in a new infection, IgA protects mucous
surfaces and IgE is involved in allergies.

## Monoclonal and polyclonal antibodies
Monoclonal antibodies all come from one B cell clone and recognise exactly the same epitope. Polyclonal antibodies
are a mixture from many B cells, usually collected from the blood of an immunised animal, and recognise several
epitopes on the same antigen. Monoclonals give consistent results, while polyclonals often give stronger signals.

## What ELISA measures
ELISA, the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, measures how much of a target molecule is in a sample using antibodies
and a colour reaction. It is done in a plastic plate with 96 small wells. ELISA can detect proteins, hormones or
antibodies at very low levels, often in the picogram to nanogram per millilitre range.

## How a sandwich ELISA works
In a sandwich ELISA the target is trapped between two antibodies. A capture antibody coated on the well grabs the
target from the sample, and after washing a second detection antibody binds another part of the target. The
detection antibody carries an enzyme that produces the signal.

## The colour reaction in ELISA
The colour in ELISA comes from an enzyme, usually horseradish peroxidase (HRP), linked to the detection antibody.
When the substrate TMB is added, HRP turns it blue, and adding acid stops the reaction and turns it yellow. A plate
reader then measures the absorbance at 450 nm, and more colour means more target.

## Blocking and washing
Blocking and washing reduce background signal in an antibody test. After coating, the wells are blocked with a
protein such as bovine serum albumin or milk powder, which covers empty plastic so antibodies cannot stick to it.
Washing three to five times between steps removes unbound material.

## The standard curve in ELISA
A standard curve turns ELISA absorbance readings into concentrations. Known amounts of the target are run in the same
plate, usually as a series of two-fold dilutions, and their absorbance is plotted against concentration. Each
sample's concentration is then read off this curve, which only works within its range.

## Western blot
A Western blot detects one specific protein in a mixture after the proteins are separated by size. The proteins are
run on an SDS-PAGE gel, transferred onto a membrane and then probed with an antibody against the target. A band at the
expected size confirms the protein is present and gives a rough idea of how much.
