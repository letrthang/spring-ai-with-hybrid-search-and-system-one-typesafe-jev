# RAS basics — Maintenance schedules

## Why a RAS needs a maintenance schedule
A maintenance schedule lists every maintenance task in a RAS with how often it is done and who is responsible.
Because a RAS depends on pumps, blowers, filters and sensors running 24 hours a day, missed maintenance quickly
turns into equipment failure and fish losses. A written schedule makes sure tasks are not forgotten when staff
change or work gets busy.

## Daily, weekly, monthly and yearly tasks
RAS maintenance tasks are usually grouped by frequency into daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly and yearly lists.
Daily tasks cover the checks and cleaning that keep the system running, while yearly tasks cover major services and
replacements. Grouping tasks this way makes it easy to plan work and to spread it evenly through the week.

## Weekly maintenance tasks
Typical weekly RAS maintenance tasks include cleaning online sensors, checking pH probe calibration, inspecting
drum filter spray nozzles and cleaning tank walls and outlet screens. Many farms also run standby pumps briefly and
inspect the oxygen cone and diffusers for blockages each week. Weekly tasks catch small problems before they become
emergencies.

## Monthly maintenance tasks
Monthly RAS maintenance often includes calibrating dissolved oxygen sensors, testing alarm notifications, cleaning
UV quartz sleeves, checking pump motor current and vibration, and testing the backup generator under load. Monthly
tasks are also a good time to check stock levels of spare parts, buffers and chemicals. Records of each task help
show trends in equipment condition.

## Yearly maintenance tasks
Yearly RAS maintenance usually includes replacing UV lamps, servicing pumps and blowers, replacing worn seals and
bearings, and inspecting heat exchangers. UV lamps lose output after about one year of continuous use (around
9,000–12,000 hours). Optical DO sensor caps and pH probes are often replaced every one to two years. Major yearly
work is best planned between batches or when biomass is low.

## Preventive versus reactive maintenance
Preventive maintenance means servicing equipment on a fixed schedule before it fails, while reactive maintenance
means repairing equipment only after it breaks. In a RAS, reactive maintenance is risky because a failed pump or
oxygen supply can kill fish within an hour. Most farms aim for preventive maintenance on all critical equipment and
accept reactive repairs only for non-critical items.

## Keeping a stock of spare parts
A RAS should keep spare parts on site for every critical item, such as a spare pump or pump motor, a blower, sensor
caps, pH probes, UV lamps, drum filter nozzles and fuses. Waiting days for a part from a supplier can force a farm
to run without redundancy. A spare parts list linked to the maintenance schedule shows what to reorder after each
use.

## Maintenance records and logs
Every maintenance task in a RAS should be recorded with the date, the person who did it, what was found and any
parts replaced. Maintenance records show which equipment fails often and help plan replacements before failures
happen. Records are also useful for audits, certification and warranty claims.

## Doing maintenance without stressing fish
Maintenance near fish tanks should be done calmly and at times that disturb the fish least, such as after feeding
is finished. Work that stops flow or oxygen to a tank, such as changing a pump, must have a backup in place before
starting. Staff should avoid loud noise, sudden light changes and chemical spills during maintenance.

## Using software to manage maintenance
Maintenance software or a simple shared spreadsheet can schedule RAS tasks, send reminders and record when each job
is done. Running hours from pumps and UV lamps can trigger maintenance automatically instead of relying only on
calendar dates. A digital system also makes it easy to see overdue tasks at a glance.
