# RAS basics — Sludge and waste handling

## How much sludge a RAS produces
A RAS produces roughly 0.25 to 0.35 kg of dry solids for every kilogram of feed fed. Most of this comes from
faeces, with a smaller share from uneaten feed and bacterial biomass. A farm feeding 1,000 kg of feed per day must
therefore handle around 300 kg of dry sludge daily, which is many cubic metres of wet sludge.

## Dry matter at each sludge stage
The dry matter content of RAS sludge rises at each treatment stage. Backwash water from a drum filter holds only
about 0.1 to 0.5 % dry matter, thickened sludge holds about 2 to 5 %, and dewatered sludge from a belt filter or
screw press holds about 10 to 25 %. Each step greatly cuts the volume of sludge that has to be stored and
transported.

## Thickening sludge by settling
Sludge thickening concentrates drum filter backwash water by letting the solids settle in a cone-bottom tank or
settling tank for a few hours. The settled sludge is pumped from the bottom, and the clearer water above is drained
off. Short settling times are better, because sludge that sits too long begins to rot and release ammonium and
phosphate.

## Polymer flocculants for dewatering
A polymer flocculant is a long-chain chemical, usually a cationic polyacrylamide, that binds fine sludge particles
into large flocs that release water easily. The polymer is dosed in small amounts just before the dewatering
machine. The right polymer dose is found with a simple jar test, because too little gives a wet sludge cake and too
much wastes money.

## Sludge dewatering equipment
Sludge dewatering equipment for RAS includes belt filters, screw presses, decanter centrifuges and geotextile bags.
Belt filters and screw presses suit small and medium farms, while centrifuges reach drier sludge but cost more to
buy and run. Geotextile bags are cheap and simple, but they need space and dewater slowly over weeks.

## Why sludge should not be stored long
RAS sludge should not be stored long, because it quickly turns anaerobic and starts to rot. Rotting sludge releases
ammonium, phosphate, odours and gases such as hydrogen sulfide and methane. Sludge tanks should be emptied often,
kept cool and kept separate from the fish loop.

## Reject water from dewatering
Reject water is the liquid squeezed out of sludge during dewatering, and it is rich in ammonium, phosphate and
organic matter. Reject water should not go back into the fish loop, because it adds a heavy waste load to the
biofilter. Farms send reject water to a wastewater treatment plant, a constructed wetland or a denitrification
reactor.

## Using fish sludge as fertiliser
Fish sludge can be used as an agricultural fertiliser, because it contains nitrogen, phosphorus and organic matter.
Sludge from marine RAS is salty, which limits how much can be spread on fields. Before use, fish sludge is often
stabilised with lime or composted, and many countries require testing for heavy metals such as zinc and copper that
come from the feed.

## Biogas from fish sludge
Fish sludge can be fed into an anaerobic digester, where bacteria turn its organic matter into biogas rich in
methane. The biogas can produce heat and electricity, and the remaining digestate can be used as fertiliser. Fish
sludge is often digested together with farm manure or food waste, because the high salt or ammonia levels of pure
fish sludge can slow the process.

## Handling dead fish
Dead fish from a RAS must be stored and disposed of safely to avoid spreading disease. A common method is ensilage,
where mortalities are ground and mixed with formic acid to keep the pH below about 4 so they do not rot. The
ensiled fish are then collected for rendering, biogas or incineration according to local rules.
