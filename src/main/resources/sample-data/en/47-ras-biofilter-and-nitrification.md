# RAS basics — Biofilter and nitrification

## What a biofilter does
A biofilter turns toxic ammonia from fish waste into nitrate, which is far less harmful to fish. It is a tank full of
plastic media on which nitrifying bacteria grow as a thin film. Water from the fish tanks flows past this film, and
the bacteria use the ammonia as their energy source.

## The nitrification process
Nitrification is a two-step process in which bacteria first turn ammonia into nitrite and then turn nitrite into
nitrate. Ammonia-oxidising bacteria such as Nitrosomonas do the first step, and nitrite-oxidising bacteria such as
Nitrospira do the second. Both steps need oxygen, and the process also uses up alkalinity in the water.

## What TAN means
TAN stands for total ammonia nitrogen, the sum of ionised ammonium (NH4+) and un-ionised ammonia (NH3) in the water.
Most test kits and sensors report TAN in mg/L. In a RAS, TAN is usually kept below 1 to 2 mg/L for warm-water species
and below 0.5 to 1 mg/L for salmon and trout.

## Why un-ionised ammonia is the dangerous form
Un-ionised ammonia (NH3) is the toxic form of ammonia, because it passes easily through fish gills. The share of TAN
that is NH3 rises sharply with higher pH and temperature: at pH 7 and 20 °C it is under 1 %, but at pH 8.5 it is over
10 %. Salmonids should be kept below about 0.0125 mg/L NH3.

## Nitrite toxicity and brown blood
Nitrite is toxic because it changes haemoglobin into methaemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen, giving fish brown
blood. Freshwater fish are most at risk, and levels above 0.5 to 1 mg/L can harm trout and salmon. Adding salt helps,
because chloride competes with nitrite at the gills and reduces how much nitrite the fish absorb.

## What a moving-bed biofilter is
A moving-bed biofilter, or MBBR, is a tank filled with small plastic carriers that are kept tumbling by air bubbles
from the bottom. Bacteria grow on the protected inner surfaces of each carrier. The constant movement keeps the media
from clogging and scrubs off old biofilm, so an MBBR needs very little maintenance.

## Biofilter media surface area
Biofilter capacity depends on the protected surface area of the media, typically 500 to 800 m² per cubic metre of
media for MBBR carriers. A well-run biofilter removes about 0.2 to 0.6 g of TAN per square metre per day, depending on
temperature. The media fill is usually 50 to 65 % of the tank volume so the carriers can move freely.

## Starting up a new biofilter
A new biofilter takes four to eight weeks to build a full population of nitrifying bacteria, a process called
cycling. Farms speed it up by adding ammonium chloride and a bacterial starter culture before stocking any fish.
During this period ammonia peaks first, then nitrite, and the filter is ready when both stay near zero.

## Conditions nitrifying bacteria need
Nitrifying bacteria work best with dissolved oxygen above 4 mg/L, pH between 7.0 and 8.0 and alkalinity above
100 mg/L as CaCO3. They also work faster in warm water, roughly doubling their activity for every 10 °C rise. Sudden
changes in temperature, salinity or pH can slow them down for days.

## What harms a biofilter
Antibiotics, disinfectants, formalin and high doses of salt or copper can kill nitrifying bacteria and crash a
biofilter. A crash shows as a sudden rise in TAN or nitrite a day or two after the treatment. Fish treatments should
be done in isolated tanks or bypass the biofilter whenever possible.
