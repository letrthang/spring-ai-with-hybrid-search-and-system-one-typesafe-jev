# RAS basics — Temperature control

## Why temperature control matters in a RAS
Water temperature controls how fast fish eat, grow and use oxygen, so a RAS keeps it steady at the best value for the
species. A few degrees too cold slows growth, while too warm raises oxygen demand and disease risk. Stable
temperature also keeps the biofilter bacteria working at a steady rate.

## Ideal temperatures for common RAS species
Atlantic salmon smolts and rainbow trout grow best at about 12 to 16 °C, while tilapia and barramundi prefer 26 to
30 °C. Whiteleg shrimp grow fastest at 28 to 31 °C. A RAS lets the farm hold these temperatures all year, no matter
the outdoor weather.

## How a heat pump warms RAS water
A heat pump warms RAS water by moving heat from outdoor air, ground or waste water into the system using a
refrigerant cycle. It typically delivers 3 to 5 kWh of heat for every kWh of electricity it uses. Heat pumps are the
most common heating choice for warm-water RAS in temperate climates.

## Cooling with chillers
A chiller cools RAS water by removing heat with a refrigeration unit, through a titanium or plastic heat exchanger.
Cold-water species such as salmon and trout often need cooling, because pumps, fish metabolism and warm weather add
heat. Many modern units can switch between heating and cooling as needed.

## Where the heat in a RAS comes from
Pumps, blowers and fish metabolism all add heat to a RAS, often enough to warm the water by several degrees each day.
Almost all pump energy ends up as heat in the water. In a well-insulated building, this means a cold-water system may
need cooling even in winter.

## Avoiding sudden temperature changes
Fish should not face temperature changes of more than about 1 to 2 °C per day, and sudden shifts of 3 °C or more cause
stress. This matters most when adding make-up water, moving fish between tanks or restarting a heater after a fault.
Large changes should be made slowly over several days.

## Heat recovery from discharge water
A heat exchanger can recover heat from the warm water leaving a RAS and use it to warm incoming make-up water. In
warm-water systems with daily exchange, this can cut heating energy by half or more. The exchanger must be easy to
clean, because organic fouling quickly lowers its efficiency.

## Building insulation and humidity
A RAS building should be well insulated, because warm, humid air above the tanks loses heat to the walls and roof.
Covering tanks and ventilating with heat recovery reduces both heat loss and condensation. Condensation can damage
electrical panels, sensors and the building itself.

## Temperature alarm limits
A temperature alarm typically warns when water moves more than 1 to 2 °C from the set point and goes critical at 3 °C
or more. For salmon and trout, readings above 18 °C are a serious concern because oxygen demand rises sharply. Each
tank should have its own temperature sensor.

## Temperature and dissolved oxygen
Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen than cold water, while fish in warm water need more of it. At sea level, fresh
water holds about 10.1 mg/L of oxygen at 15 °C but only about 7.5 mg/L at 30 °C. Warm-water RAS therefore depends
heavily on pure oxygen.
