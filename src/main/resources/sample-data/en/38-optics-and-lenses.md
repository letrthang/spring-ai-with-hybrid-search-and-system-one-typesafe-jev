# Physics basics — optics and lenses

## The law of reflection
When light reflects from a smooth surface, the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, both measured from
the normal, a line at right angles to the surface. A smooth mirror gives regular reflection and a clear image, while a
rough surface such as paper scatters light in all directions, which is called diffuse reflection.

## Images in a plane mirror
A plane mirror forms an image that is upright, the same size as the object and laterally inverted, so left and right
appear swapped. The image appears as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it. It is a virtual image:
light does not actually pass through it, so it cannot be projected onto a screen.

## Refraction
Refraction is the bending of light when it passes from one material into another at an angle, because its speed
changes. Light slows down and bends towards the normal when it enters glass or water from air. That is why a straw in
a glass of water looks bent and a swimming pool looks shallower than it really is.

## Refractive index and Snell's law
The refractive index of a material is the speed of light in a vacuum divided by its speed in the material, n = c / v.
Water has n = 1.33 and glass about 1.5, so light in glass travels at about 200,000 km/s. Snell's law links the angles
on each side of a boundary: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂.

## Total internal reflection
Total internal reflection happens when light inside a denser material hits the boundary at more than the critical
angle and is all reflected back inside. For glass to air the critical angle is about 42°, and for water about 49°.
Optical fibres use total internal reflection to carry light signals for many kilometres along thin glass strands.

## Converging lenses
A converging (convex) lens is thicker in the middle and bends parallel rays of light inward to meet at a point called
the principal focus. The distance from the lens to this point is its focal length. A magnifying glass, a camera lens
and the lens of the human eye are all converging lenses.

## Diverging lenses
A diverging (concave) lens is thinner in the middle and spreads parallel rays apart, as if they came from a focus in
front of the lens. It always forms an upright, smaller, virtual image. Diverging lenses are used in glasses that
correct short sight and in door peepholes to give a wide view.

## The thin lens equation
The thin lens equation links focal length f, object distance u and image distance v: 1/f = 1/u + 1/v. For a lens with
f = 10 cm and an object 30 cm away, the image forms 15 cm behind the lens. Magnification is the image distance divided
by the object distance, here 15 / 30 = 0.5.

## Lens power and dioptres
The power of a lens is one divided by its focal length in metres, P = 1/f, measured in dioptres (D). A converging lens
with a focal length of 0.5 m has a power of +2 D, while diverging lenses have negative power. Glasses prescriptions
are written in dioptres, for example −2.5 D for a short-sighted person.

## How the eye focuses
The eye focuses light mainly with the cornea and fine-tunes it with a flexible lens that changes shape, a process
called accommodation. In short sight, distant objects focus in front of the retina; in long sight, near objects focus
behind it. A young eye can focus from about 25 cm to infinity, but the lens stiffens with age.
