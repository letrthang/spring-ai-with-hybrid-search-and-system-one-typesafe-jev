# Physics basics — electromagnetic waves

## The electromagnetic spectrum
The electromagnetic spectrum, from longest to shortest wavelength, runs from radio waves through microwaves, infrared,
visible light and ultraviolet to X-rays and gamma rays. All are transverse waves of oscillating electric and magnetic
fields and travel through a vacuum at the same speed. Shorter wavelengths have higher frequencies and carry more
energy per photon.

## Radio waves
Radio waves have wavelengths from around a metre to many kilometres and are produced by electrons oscillating in an
antenna. FM radio broadcasts at about 88 to 108 MHz, with wavelengths near 3 m, while long-wave AM signals can bend
around hills and follow Earth's curve. Radio waves also carry TV and two-way radio signals.

## Microwaves
Microwaves have wavelengths from about 1 mm to 1 m. A microwave oven uses 2.45 GHz waves, which make water molecules
in food rotate and heat up. Microwaves also carry Wi-Fi, mobile phone and satellite signals, because they pass easily
through the atmosphere and can be sent in narrow beams.

## Infrared
Infrared radiation has wavelengths just longer than red light and is given off by all warm objects; the human body
radiates most strongly at about 10 µm. Thermal cameras detect it to make images in the dark, TV remote controls send
infrared pulses, and grills and heat lamps use it to cook and warm.

## Ultraviolet
Ultraviolet (UV) light has wavelengths shorter than violet light, from about 10 to 400 nm. It causes sunburn, helps
the skin make vitamin D and can damage DNA, raising the risk of skin cancer. UV makes fluorescent materials glow and
is used to sterilise water and surfaces. The ozone layer absorbs most of the Sun's harmful UV.

## X-rays
X-rays are high-energy electromagnetic waves with wavelengths of about 0.01 to 10 nm. They pass easily through soft
tissue but are absorbed by dense materials such as bone and metal, so bones show up white on an X-ray image. Because
X-rays are ionising, staff stand behind lead screens and patient doses are kept as low as possible.

## Photons and photon energy
Electromagnetic radiation comes in packets of energy called photons, each with energy E = h f, where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴
J·s is Planck's constant. A photon of green light at 5.6 × 10¹⁴ Hz carries about 3.7 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, or 2.3 eV.
Higher-frequency photons, such as UV and X-rays, carry enough energy to break chemical bonds.

## Ionising and non-ionising radiation
Radiation is ionising if its photons have enough energy to knock electrons out of atoms. Short-wavelength ultraviolet,
X-rays and gamma rays are ionising and can damage living cells and DNA. Radio waves, microwaves, infrared and visible
light are non-ionising: they can heat tissue but cannot break molecules apart in this way.

## Polarisation
Polarisation means the electric field of a wave vibrates in only one direction. Ordinary light vibrates in all
directions, but a polarising filter lets through only one. Light reflected from water or a road is partly polarised
horizontally, so polarised sunglasses with vertical filters cut glare. Only transverse waves such as light can be
polarised; sound cannot.

## The inverse-square law for radiation
The intensity of radiation from a point source falls with the square of the distance, I ∝ 1 / r². Moving twice as far
from a lamp or a radio transmitter leaves a quarter of the intensity, and three times as far leaves a ninth. This
happens because the same energy spreads over a sphere whose area grows as 4π r².
