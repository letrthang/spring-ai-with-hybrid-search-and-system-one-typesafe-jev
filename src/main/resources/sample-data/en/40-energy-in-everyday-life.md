# Physics basics — energy in everyday life

## Energy stores and transfers
Energy is held in stores such as chemical (food, fuel, batteries), kinetic, gravitational, elastic, thermal and
nuclear. It moves between stores by heating, by forces doing work, by electric currents and by radiation such as light
and sound. When a phone charges, an electric current fills the chemical store of its battery.

## Efficiency
Efficiency is the fraction of input energy that ends up as useful output: efficiency = useful output ÷ total input ×
100%. An LED bulb turns around 30 to 40% of its electrical energy into light, while an old filament bulb manages only
about 5%. The wasted energy usually ends up as heat spread into the surroundings.

## Energy in food
Food energy is measured in kilojoules or kilocalories, where 1 kcal (one dietary Calorie) equals about 4.2 kJ. An
adult needs roughly 8,000 to 10,000 kJ a day, about 2,000 to 2,400 kcal. A 100 g chocolate bar holds about 2,200 kJ,
enough to run a 60 W light bulb for about 10 hours.

## Power ratings of household appliances
The power rating of an appliance tells how much energy it uses each second. A phone charger uses about 5 to 20 W, a
laptop around 50 W, a fridge 100 to 150 W while running, a hair dryer 1,500 to 2,000 W and an electric shower up to
10,000 W. Appliances that heat things almost always have the highest power ratings.

## Standby power
Standby power is the electricity an appliance uses while switched off but still plugged in, waiting for a remote
control or showing a clock. Modern devices usually draw less than 1 W on standby, but older ones could draw 5 to 10 W.
A 5 W standby load left on all year uses about 44 kWh.

## Home insulation and U-values
The U-value of a wall, roof or window is the heat in watts lost through each square metre for every degree of
temperature difference, in W/(m²·K). Lower U-values mean better insulation. Single glazing has a U-value of about 5,
modern double glazing about 1.4 and triple glazing about 0.8, so better windows cut heat loss several times over.

## Renewable and non-renewable sources
Renewable energy sources, such as sunlight, wind, flowing water and geothermal heat, are naturally replaced and will
not run out. Non-renewable sources, such as coal, oil, natural gas and uranium, exist in limited amounts and are used
up. Burning fossil fuels also releases carbon dioxide, which adds to global warming.

## Solar panels
A solar panel turns sunlight directly into electricity using photovoltaic cells made of silicon. Typical home panels
convert about 18 to 22% of the sunlight falling on them. On a clear day sunlight delivers about 1,000 W per square
metre, so one square metre of panel produces roughly 200 W in full sun.

## Wind turbines
A wind turbine converts the kinetic energy of moving air into electricity. The power available rises with the cube of
the wind speed, so doubling the wind speed gives eight times the power. No turbine can capture more than 59% of the
wind's energy, a limit known as the Betz limit, and large modern turbines are rated at 3 to 15 MW.

## The energy cost of heating water
Heating water uses a lot of energy because water has a high specific heat capacity. A 10-minute shower using 80 litres
of water heated from 15 °C to 40 °C needs about 8.4 MJ, or 2.3 kWh. Cutting shower time or temperature is one of the
simplest ways to reduce a household energy bill.
