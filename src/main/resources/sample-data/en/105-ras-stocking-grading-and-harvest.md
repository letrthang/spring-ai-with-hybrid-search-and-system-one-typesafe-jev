# RAS basics — Stocking, grading and harvest

## Planning a stocking schedule
A RAS farm plans its stocking schedule so that total biomass and daily feed stay close to the design capacity all
year. Many farms stock a new batch every few weeks or months instead of filling all tanks at once. Staggered stocking
gives a steady harvest for customers and a steady load on the biofilter.

## Acclimatising new fish
New fish must be acclimatised to the RAS water before release, because sudden changes in temperature, salinity or pH
cause stress. The transport water and tank water should differ by no more than about 1 to 2 °C and a few ppt of
salinity, and larger differences are evened out slowly over an hour or more. CO2 and pH in the transport water should
also be checked, since CO2 often builds up during long trips.

## Counting fish at stocking
Fish are usually counted at stocking by weighing the whole batch and dividing by the average weight of a weighed
sample. Optical or electronic fish counters give a more accurate number for small fish and avoid extra handling. An
accurate starting count is the basis for feed planning, survival records and the feed conversion ratio of the batch.

## Splitting fish into more tanks as they grow
As fish grow, the biomass in each tank rises until the density reaches the farm limit, for example about 60 to 80
kg/m³. The fish are then split into more tanks or moved to larger tanks further down the production line. Planning
these splits in advance prevents crowding, poor water quality and slower growth.

## Grading equipment
RAS farms grade fish with bar graders, in which fish swim or are pumped through gaps of a set width so smaller fish
pass and larger fish are held back. Some farms use passive graders placed inside the tank, while others pump fish
through a mechanical grader with several adjustable channels. Bar graders sort fish by body width, which matches
weight closely within one species.

## How to grade fish safely
Fish are graded safely by stopping feed about 24 hours before, lowering the water level and crowding the fish slowly.
Dissolved oxygen should be kept high during grading, often with extra pure oxygen, because crowded fish use more
oxygen. Grading in short sessions and returning fish quickly to tanks with stable water reduces stress and scale
loss.

## Moving fish with fish pumps
Fish pumps move fish through pipes with plenty of water, so the fish are not lifted out with nets. Vacuum pumps and
impeller fish pumps are both used, and a dewatering box removes most water before fish enter a grader or transport
tank. Pipes should have smooth joints and gentle bends to avoid injuries.

## Preparing fish for harvest
Fish are prepared for harvest by withholding feed for about 1 to 3 days so the gut is empty, or longer when they must
be purged for off-flavour. An empty gut reduces contamination during processing and lowers oxygen use while the fish
are crowded. Water quality checks and gentle crowding before harvest also improve fillet quality.

## Humane slaughter at harvest
Good harvest practice is to stun fish instantly, usually by percussive or electrical stunning, and then bleed them by
cutting the gill arches. Fish should be unconscious within a second and stay unconscious until death. After bleeding,
the fish are chilled quickly to about 0 to 2 °C in ice slurry to keep fresh quality.

## Harvest records for each batch
At harvest the farm records the number of fish, total weight and average weight for each batch. These numbers are
compared with the stocking count and the feed used to calculate survival, growth and the feed conversion ratio of the
batch. After harvest the tanks are cleaned and disinfected before the next batch is stocked.
