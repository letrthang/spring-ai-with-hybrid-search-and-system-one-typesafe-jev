# RAS basics — pH and alkalinity

## Target pH range in a RAS
Most freshwater RAS farms keep pH between 6.8 and 7.5, while marine and shrimp systems run between 7.5 and 8.3. This
range balances two needs: the biofilter works better at higher pH, but toxic un-ionised ammonia also rises with pH.
A steady pH matters more than the exact value.

## Why pH falls in a RAS
pH falls in a RAS mainly because nitrification produces acid and fish breathe out carbon dioxide. The nitrifying
bacteria release hydrogen ions as they turn ammonia into nitrate. Without regular buffer dosing, the pH of a
heavily fed RAS can drop by several tenths of a unit in a single day.

## What alkalinity means
Alkalinity is the water's ability to neutralise acid, mostly provided by bicarbonate and carbonate, and it is
reported in mg/L as CaCO3. It acts as a buffer that keeps pH from swinging. Most RAS farms aim for an alkalinity of
100 to 200 mg/L as CaCO3.

## How nitrification consumes alkalinity
Nitrification consumes about 7.1 g of alkalinity, as CaCO3, for every gram of ammonia nitrogen the bacteria convert.
In practice this means a RAS loses roughly 0.15 to 0.25 kg of alkalinity for each kilogram of feed. The lost
alkalinity must be replaced every day to keep pH stable.

## Dosing sodium bicarbonate
Sodium bicarbonate, or baking soda, is the most common chemical used to restore alkalinity in a RAS. It is safe, easy
to dissolve and raises alkalinity without causing sharp pH spikes. A typical dose is about 0.25 kg of sodium
bicarbonate for every kilogram of feed given.

## Other buffers: lime and sodium hydroxide
Hydrated lime and sodium hydroxide raise pH and alkalinity faster and more cheaply than sodium bicarbonate, but they
are strong bases. An overdose can push pH above 9 in minutes and kill fish. They must be dosed slowly through an
automatic pump controlled by a pH sensor, never poured into a tank by hand.

## Automatic pH control
Automatic pH control uses an in-line pH sensor and a dosing pump that adds buffer whenever pH falls below a set point,
such as 7.0. The dose should go into the sump or biofilter inlet, where it mixes well before reaching the fish. A
maximum daily dose limit protects against overdosing if the sensor fails.

## Measuring alkalinity
Alkalinity is measured by titration, adding acid to a water sample until the pH drops to about 4.5, with a test kit
or lab titrator. Most farms test alkalinity once or twice a week. A falling trend shows that buffer dosing is too low
for the current feed load.

## Effect of pH on ammonia toxicity
Higher pH makes ammonia much more toxic, because more of the total ammonia exists as un-ionised NH3. Each rise of one
pH unit increases the toxic share about tenfold. A sudden pH increase, for example from a buffer overdose, can turn
a safe TAN level into a dangerous one.

## pH and CO2 working together
In a RAS, pH depends on both alkalinity and dissolved CO2: more alkalinity raises pH, and more CO2 lowers it. If pH
is low but alkalinity is normal, the cause is usually high CO2 and the degasser should be checked. If both pH and
alkalinity are low, more buffer is needed.
