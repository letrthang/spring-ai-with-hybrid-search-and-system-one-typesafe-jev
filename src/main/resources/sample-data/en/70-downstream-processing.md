# Biotech basics — downstream processing and purification

## What downstream processing is
Downstream processing is the series of steps that recovers and purifies a product after it has been made in a
bioreactor. Downstream processing typically includes harvest, capture, purification, polishing and formulation,
turning a crude broth into a pure, concentrated and stable drug substance. For many biopharmaceuticals, downstream
processing accounts for half or more of the total production cost.

## Harvest and clarification
Harvest and clarification separate cells and debris from the liquid that contains the product. Large plants use a
disc-stack centrifuge spinning at several thousand times gravity, followed by depth filters that trap fine particles.
A final 0.2 µm filter gives a clear liquid, called the clarified harvest, that will not clog the chromatography
columns downstream.

## Cell disruption and inclusion bodies
Cell disruption is needed when the product stays inside the cells, as with many proteins made in E. coli.
High-pressure homogenisers force the cell suspension through a narrow valve at about 500–1,500 bar, and bead mills
grind cells with tiny glass beads. Many recombinant proteins in E. coli form insoluble clumps called inclusion
bodies, which are washed, dissolved in strong denaturants such as 6 M guanidine or 8 M urea, and then refolded into
the active shape.

## Protein A affinity chromatography
Protein A affinity chromatography is the standard capture step for purifying monoclonal antibodies. Protein A, a
protein from the bacterium Staphylococcus aureus, binds the Fc region of IgG at neutral pH, so the antibody sticks to
the resin while most impurities flow through. Eluting with a buffer near pH 3–3.5 releases the antibody, often at
above 95 % purity after this single step.

## Ion-exchange chromatography
Ion-exchange chromatography separates proteins by their electric charge. In cation exchange the resin carries
negative groups that bind positively charged proteins, and in anion exchange the resin carries positive groups that
bind negatively charged proteins and DNA. Bound proteins are released by raising the salt concentration or changing
the pH, and anion exchange is widely used to remove DNA, host cell proteins and viruses from antibody products.

## Polishing steps
Polishing steps remove the last traces of impurities and unwanted product variants after the capture step.
Hydrophobic interaction chromatography separates proteins by surface hydrophobicity using a falling salt gradient and
is good at removing aggregates. Size-exclusion chromatography sorts molecules by size as they pass through porous
beads, separating antibody monomers of about 150 kDa from dimers and larger aggregates.

## Viral clearance
Viral clearance steps make sure that any virus coming from cell lines or raw materials is inactivated or removed. A
low-pH hold, typically around pH 3.5 for 30–60 minutes, inactivates enveloped viruses, and solvent-detergent
treatment works in a similar way. Virus filters rated at about 20 nm physically remove even small viruses, and each
step is validated by spiking in model viruses and measuring the log reduction.

## Ultrafiltration and diafiltration
Ultrafiltration and diafiltration (UF/DF) concentrate the product and exchange it into its final buffer. In
tangential flow filtration the liquid sweeps across a membrane, and a membrane with a 30 kDa cut-off lets water,
salts and small molecules pass while holding back a 150 kDa antibody. During diafiltration new buffer is added as
fast as filtrate is removed, and about 7 diavolumes replace more than 99.9 % of the old buffer.

## Impurities and purity targets
A purified biopharmaceutical must meet strict limits for several kinds of impurity. Host cell proteins are usually
kept below about 100 ppm, meaning 100 ng per mg of product, and residual host cell DNA is commonly limited to 10 ng
per dose. Endotoxin, leached Protein A, aggregates and leftover process chemicals are also measured, using methods
such as ELISA, qPCR and the LAL test.

## Step yield and overall yield
The overall yield of a downstream process is the product of the yields of all its individual steps. Five steps at 90
% each give 0.9⁵, or about 59 % overall, so every extra step costs product as well as time and money. Process
developers therefore keep the number of steps low, and a typical antibody purification process recovers about 60–80 %
of the antibody in the clarified harvest.
