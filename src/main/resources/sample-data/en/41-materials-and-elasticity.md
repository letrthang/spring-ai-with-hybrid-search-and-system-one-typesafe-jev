# Physics basics — materials and elasticity

## Hooke's law
Hooke's law says the extension of a spring is proportional to the force stretching it, F = k · x, where k is the
spring constant in newtons per metre. A spring with k = 200 N/m stretches 5 cm under a 10 N load. The law holds only
up to the limit of proportionality, beyond which the spring stretches more than expected.

## Elastic and plastic deformation
Elastic deformation is a change of shape that disappears when the force is removed, like a stretched rubber band
springing back. Plastic deformation is permanent: the material stays bent or stretched, like a bent paperclip. The
point where a material stops behaving elastically is called its elastic limit.

## Stress
Stress is the force acting on a material divided by the cross-sectional area it acts over, σ = F / A, measured in
pascals. A 1,000 N load hanging from a wire with a cross-section of 1 mm² (10⁻⁶ m²) puts it under a stress of 10⁹ Pa,
or 1 GPa. A thicker wire spreads the same load over more area and has a lower stress.

## Strain
Strain is the fractional change in length of a material, the extension divided by the original length, ε = ΔL / L. It
has no units. A 2 m wire that stretches by 1 mm has a strain of 0.0005, or 0.05%. Most metals can only be strained by
a fraction of a percent before they deform permanently.

## Young's modulus
Young's modulus measures a material's stiffness: it is stress divided by strain, E = σ / ε, in pascals. Steel has a
Young's modulus of about 200 GPa, aluminium about 70 GPa, and rubber only about 0.01 to 0.1 GPa. A higher value means
the material stretches less under the same stress.

## Tensile strength
Tensile strength is the maximum stress a material can take when stretched before it breaks. Structural steel has an
ultimate tensile strength of about 400 to 550 MPa, aluminium alloys 200 to 600 MPa, and spider silk over 1,000 MPa.
Engineers design parts to work far below this value, using a safety factor.

## Elastic potential energy
A stretched or compressed spring stores elastic potential energy, E = ½ k x². A spring with k = 400 N/m stretched by
0.1 m stores ½ × 400 × 0.01 = 2 J. This stored energy is released when the spring returns to its natural length, as in
a bow, a trampoline or a wind-up toy.

## Brittle and ductile materials
A brittle material, such as glass or cast iron, breaks suddenly with little or no plastic deformation. A ductile
material, such as copper or mild steel, stretches a long way and can be drawn into wires before it breaks. Ductile
materials give a warning by bending before they fail, which is why they are preferred for structures.

## Thermal expansion
Most materials expand when heated: ΔL = α · L · ΔT, where α is the coefficient of linear expansion. Steel has an α of
about 12 × 10⁻⁶ per °C, so a 100 m steel bridge grows about 3.6 cm when it warms by 30 °C. Bridges and railway tracks
have expansion gaps to allow for this.

## Hardness
Hardness is a material's resistance to being scratched or dented. The Mohs scale ranks minerals from 1 (talc) to 10
(diamond), and a material can scratch anything with a lower number. A fingernail is about 2.5, glass about 5.5 and
quartz 7, which is why sand, which is mostly quartz, can scratch glass.
