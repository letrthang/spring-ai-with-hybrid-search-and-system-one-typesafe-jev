# Biotech basics — lab safety and sterile technique

## Personal protective equipment
Personal protective equipment (PPE) is the clothing and gear that protect you from lab hazards. The basics are a
buttoned lab coat, safety glasses and disposable gloves, usually nitrile. Gloves should be changed when torn or
contaminated and removed before touching door handles, phones or keyboards.

## Biosafety levels
Biosafety levels (BSL-1 to BSL-4) set how strict the containment must be for a given microbe. BSL-1 covers harmless
organisms such as lab strains of E. coli, while BSL-2 is for moderate-risk agents such as Salmonella. BSL-3 and BSL-4
are for dangerous airborne pathogens and need special buildings and training.

## Biosafety cabinets
A biosafety cabinet is an enclosed workspace that uses filtered airflow to protect the user, the sample and the
room. Air is cleaned through HEPA filters, which remove at least 99.97% of particles 0.3 µm in size. Work should be
done at least 10 cm inside the front opening, without blocking the air grilles.

## Sterile technique
Sterile technique, also called aseptic technique, is a set of habits that keep unwanted microbes out of cultures and
media. It means working in a clean area, opening containers only briefly, never touching sterile tips or rims, and
keeping lids face down. Good technique protects both the experiment and the worker.

## The autoclave
An autoclave sterilises equipment and waste with pressurised steam. A standard cycle runs at 121 °C and about 15 psi
(1 bar) above atmospheric pressure for at least 15–20 minutes, which kills bacteria, viruses and even spores. Larger
loads or liquid volumes need longer times so the heat reaches the centre.

## Disinfecting surfaces
Benches are wiped with a disinfectant before and after work to kill microbes on the surface. 70% ethanol is the most
common choice because some water helps it get into cells and it evaporates slowly enough to act. A fresh 10% bleach
solution is used for spills of infectious material.

## The flame and the loop
A Bunsen burner or loop sterilizer is used to sterilise a metal inoculating loop before and after each use. The loop
is heated until it glows red, then left to cool for a few seconds so it does not kill the cells it picks up. Flames
must never be used inside a biosafety cabinet.

## Handling chemical spills
A chemical spill is handled by first warning people nearby and checking the safety data sheet (SDS) for that
chemical. Small spills are covered with a suitable absorbent while wearing gloves and eye protection. Large spills or
spills of very toxic chemicals mean leaving the area and calling the safety team.

## Sharps and biological waste
Sharps such as needles, scalpel blades and broken glass go into a rigid, puncture-proof sharps container. Needles
should never be recapped by hand. Biological waste, such as used cultures and plates, goes into marked biohazard bags
and is autoclaved before it leaves the lab.

## Eye wash and emergencies
If a chemical splashes in your eyes, go straight to the eye wash station and rinse for at least 15 minutes, holding
the eyelids open. For large splashes on the body, use the safety shower and remove contaminated clothing. Every
accident, even a small one, should be reported to the lab supervisor.
