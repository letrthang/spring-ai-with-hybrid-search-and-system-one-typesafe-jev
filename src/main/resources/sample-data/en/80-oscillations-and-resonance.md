# Physics basics — oscillations and resonance

## What an oscillation is
An oscillation is a repeated back-and-forth motion around a central rest position, called the equilibrium position.
A swinging pendulum, a mass bouncing on a spring, a vibrating guitar string and the quartz crystal in a watch all
oscillate. An oscillation needs a restoring force that always pulls the object back toward equilibrium, plus
inertia that carries the object past it.

## Period, frequency and amplitude
The period T of an oscillation is the time for one complete cycle, and the frequency f is the number of cycles per
second, measured in hertz (Hz): f = 1 / T. A pendulum with a period of 2 s has a frequency of 0.5 Hz, while a
quartz watch crystal oscillates at 32,768 Hz. The amplitude is the largest distance the object moves away from
equilibrium.

## Simple harmonic motion
Simple harmonic motion, or SHM, is an oscillation in which the restoring force is proportional to the displacement
and points back toward equilibrium: F = −k · x. A graph of position against time in SHM is a smooth sine wave. The
object moves fastest as it passes the equilibrium position and stops for an instant at each end of its swing, where
the acceleration is greatest.

## The mass on a spring
A mass on a spring oscillates with a period that depends only on the mass and the spring's stiffness: T = 2π √(m /
k), where k is the spring constant in N/m. A 0.5 kg mass on a spring with k = 50 N/m has T = 2π √0.01 ≈ 0.63 s. A
heavier mass makes the oscillation slower, a stiffer spring makes it faster, and the amplitude does not change the
period.

## The simple pendulum
A simple pendulum, a small weight on a light string, has a period that depends only on its length and gravity: T =
2π √(L / g), for small swings of less than about 15°. A 1 m pendulum on Earth has a period of about 2.0 s, and
making it four times longer doubles the period. The mass of the weight does not matter, which is why pendulums made
reliable clocks for nearly 300 years.

## Energy in an oscillator
An oscillator constantly swaps energy between kinetic energy and potential energy, while the total stays the same
if there is no friction. A pendulum has the most kinetic energy at the bottom of its swing and the most potential
energy at each end, where it briefly stops. The total energy of a spring oscillator is ½ k A², so doubling the
amplitude A stores four times as much energy.

## Damping
Damping is the gradual loss of energy from an oscillation, usually to friction or air resistance, which makes the
amplitude shrink over time. A swing left alone slowly comes to rest because of light damping. Car shock absorbers
add strong damping on purpose, close to critical damping, so that after a bump the car returns to its normal height
quickly without bouncing up and down.

## Natural frequency and forced oscillations
The natural frequency is the frequency at which an object oscillates when it is disturbed and then left alone. A
forced oscillation happens when an outside force keeps pushing the object at a set rate, called the driving
frequency, such as an engine shaking a car body or a person pushing a child on a swing. After a short time the
object vibrates at the driving frequency, not at its natural frequency.

## Resonance
Resonance is the large build-up of amplitude that happens when the driving frequency matches an object's natural
frequency. Each push then arrives in step with the motion and adds energy every cycle, which is why small,
well-timed pushes can send a swing very high. The less damping a system has, the taller and sharper its resonance
peak, and a singer holding the right note can shatter a thin wine glass this way.

## Resonance in engineering
Resonance in engineering can be useful or dangerous. Radios use electrical resonance to tune in to one station's
frequency, and MRI scanners rely on the resonance of hydrogen nuclei in a magnetic field. Unwanted resonance can
damage buildings and bridges, so engineers add dampers: the Taipei 101 skyscraper hangs a 660-tonne steel ball near
its top to reduce swaying in strong wind and earthquakes.
