# Biotech basics — cloning and plasmids

## What gene cloning is
Gene cloning is making many identical copies of a gene by putting it into a carrier DNA and growing it in a host
cell. The carrier, called a vector, is usually a plasmid, and the host is usually the bacterium E. coli. Each time the
bacteria divide, they copy the plasmid and the gene inside it.

## What a plasmid is
A plasmid is a small, circular piece of DNA that lives in a bacterium separately from its chromosome. Natural
plasmids often carry useful genes, such as antibiotic resistance. Lab plasmids are typically 2,000–10,000 base pairs
long and are designed to be easy to cut, join and copy.

## Parts of a cloning vector
A cloning vector has three key parts. The origin of replication (ori) lets the plasmid be copied inside the host; a
selectable marker, often an antibiotic resistance gene, lets only cells with the plasmid survive. The multiple
cloning site is a short stretch with many unique restriction sites for inserting the gene.

## Restriction enzymes
A restriction enzyme is a protein that cuts DNA at a specific short sequence, usually 4–8 base pairs long. EcoRI, for
example, cuts at GAATTC. Many restriction enzymes cut the two strands at slightly different places, leaving short
single-stranded overhangs called sticky ends.

## Sticky ends and blunt ends
Sticky ends are short single-stranded overhangs that can pair with matching overhangs cut by the same enzyme. This
makes it easy to join a gene into a plasmid in the right orientation. Some enzymes cut straight across both strands
and leave blunt ends, which can be joined to any other blunt end but less efficiently.

## DNA ligase
DNA ligase is the enzyme that joins two pieces of DNA by sealing the sugar-phosphate backbone. In cloning, T4 DNA
ligase links the cut gene to the cut plasmid to make one closed circle, called a recombinant plasmid. Ligation is
often done at 16 °C overnight or at room temperature for 10–60 minutes.

## Transformation
Transformation is putting a plasmid into a bacterial cell. In heat shock, competent E. coli cells are mixed with the
plasmid on ice, heated to 42 °C for about 30–45 seconds and returned to ice. Electroporation instead uses a short
electric pulse to open temporary pores in the cell membrane.

## Selecting transformed cells
After transformation, cells are spread on agar plates containing an antibiotic such as ampicillin. Only bacteria that
took up the plasmid carry the resistance gene and can grow into colonies. Usually only a tiny fraction of cells, often
less than 1%, takes up a plasmid, so selection is essential.

## Blue-white screening
Blue-white screening shows which colonies carry a plasmid with an inserted gene. The plasmid's lacZ gene makes an
enzyme that turns the chemical X-gal blue. When a gene is inserted into lacZ it breaks the enzyme, so colonies with the
insert stay white while those without it turn blue.

## Checking a clone
A clone is checked to make sure it carries the right gene in the right form. Common checks are colony PCR across the
insert, cutting the purified plasmid with restriction enzymes and looking at the fragment sizes on a gel, and finally
Sanger sequencing. Sequencing confirms that the gene has no unwanted mutations.
