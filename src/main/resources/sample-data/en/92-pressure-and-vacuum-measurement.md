# Physics basics — measuring pressure and vacuum

## Units of pressure
Pressure is measured in pascals (Pa), where 1 Pa is a force of 1 newton on 1 square metre, but several other units
are common. One standard atmosphere is 101,325 Pa, which equals 1.013 bar, 760 mmHg or about 14.7 psi. Weather maps
use hectopascals (hPa), tyre gauges often show bar or psi, and vacuum work uses millibar (mbar) or torr, where 1
torr is about 133 Pa.

## The U-tube manometer
A U-tube manometer measures pressure as the height difference between two liquid levels in a bent glass or plastic
tube. The pressure difference is Δp = ρ g h, so a 10 cm difference in a water-filled tube equals about 981 Pa,
roughly 1 kPa. Water manometers suit small pressures such as gas lines and air ducts, while denser mercury was used
for higher pressures because it gives a shorter column.

## The Bourdon tube gauge
A Bourdon tube gauge is the classic dial pressure gauge, built around a curved, flattened metal tube that tries to
straighten when the pressure inside rises. A small gear linkage turns this movement into the rotation of a needle
across a scale. Bourdon gauges need no power, cover ranges from below 1 bar to over 1,000 bar, and are found on
compressors, gas cylinders and hydraulic machines.

## Barometers
A barometer measures atmospheric pressure, which is about 1,013 hPa at sea level on an average day. A mercury
barometer balances the air pressure against a column of mercury about 760 mm high, while an aneroid barometer uses
a sealed, flexible metal capsule that squeezes and expands. Near sea level the pressure falls by about 12 hPa for
every 100 m of height, which is how altimeters estimate altitude.

## Electronic pressure sensors
An electronic pressure sensor, or pressure transducer, converts pressure into an electrical signal that a computer
can record. Most pressure transducers use a thin silicon or steel diaphragm with strain gauges in a Wheatstone
bridge, while others measure a change in capacitance as the diaphragm bends. Industrial transducers commonly output
4 to 20 mA or 0 to 10 V, and the same principle appears in smartphones, car engines and medical devices.

## Differential pressure
Differential pressure is the difference between the pressures at two points, measured with a sensor that has two
ports. A rising pressure drop across a filter shows that the filter is clogging, and the pressure drop across an
orifice plate is used to calculate flow rate. Differential pressure sensors also measure liquid level in tanks and
check that clean rooms stay at a slightly higher pressure than corridors.

## Measuring blood pressure
Blood pressure is measured in millimetres of mercury (mmHg) as two numbers: the systolic pressure when the heart
beats and the diastolic pressure between beats. A cuff around the upper arm is inflated to stop blood flow and
slowly released, while a sensor or stethoscope detects when flow restarts and when it becomes steady. A typical
healthy adult reading is about 120/80 mmHg, which is a gauge pressure above atmospheric pressure.

## What counts as a vacuum
A vacuum is any space where the gas pressure is below atmospheric pressure, and a vacuum is always stated as an
absolute pressure. Common ranges are rough vacuum from 1,000 to 1 mbar, medium vacuum from 1 to 10⁻³ mbar, high
vacuum from 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁷ mbar and ultra-high vacuum below 10⁻⁷ mbar. A vacuum cleaner only lowers the pressure
slightly, within the rough range, while electron microscopes need high vacuum.

## Vacuum gauges
Vacuum gauges measure very low pressures, and different types are needed for different ranges. A capacitance
diaphragm gauge measures the bending of a thin diaphragm and is accurate in the rough and medium range
regardless of gas type. A Pirani gauge senses how well the remaining gas carries heat away from a hot wire and
works from about 10 mbar down to 10⁻³ mbar, while an ionisation gauge counts ionised gas molecules to read high
and ultra-high vacuum.

## Vacuum pumps
A vacuum pump removes gas molecules from a sealed chamber to lower its pressure. A rotary vane pump, the common
workhorse, sweeps gas out with sliding vanes and reaches about 10⁻³ mbar. To reach high vacuum, a turbomolecular
pump with blades spinning at tens of thousands of revolutions per minute is backed by a rotary vane pump, reaching
10⁻⁸ mbar or lower for electron microscopes and thin-film coating machines.
