# Physics basics — gravity and orbits

## Newton's law of universal gravitation
Every mass attracts every other mass with a force F = G m₁ m₂ / r², where G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg². The force grows
with both masses and weakens with the square of the distance between their centres. Doubling the distance cuts the
gravitational pull to one quarter.

## Gravitational field strength
Gravitational field strength g is the force of gravity on each kilogram of mass, measured in newtons per kilogram. At
Earth's surface g is about 9.8 N/kg, on the Moon 1.6 N/kg, and on Jupiter about 25 N/kg. Because 1 N/kg equals 1 m/s²,
g is also the acceleration of a freely falling object.

## How gravity changes with height
Gravity gets weaker with height above Earth, following the inverse-square law measured from Earth's centre. At the
International Space Station, about 400 km up, g is still about 8.7 m/s², roughly 90% of its surface value. At twice
Earth's radius from the centre, about 6,400 km above the surface, g falls to a quarter, about 2.5 m/s².

## Why astronauts float
Astronauts in orbit feel weightless because they and their spacecraft are falling around Earth together, not because
gravity is missing. Both accelerate towards Earth at the same rate, so nothing presses the astronaut against the
floor. The same feeling happens briefly at the top of a roller-coaster hill or in a falling lift.

## What keeps a satellite in orbit
A satellite stays in orbit because it moves sideways so fast that, as it falls towards Earth, the curved surface drops
away beneath it. Gravity supplies the centripetal force, so G M m / r² = m v² / r, which gives the orbital speed v =
√(G M / r). In low Earth orbit this speed is about 7.8 km/s.

## Orbital period
The orbital period is the time a satellite takes to go once around its orbit, and it gets longer the higher the orbit.
The International Space Station, about 400 km up, circles Earth every 92 minutes. The Moon, about 384,000 km away,
takes about 27.3 days to complete one orbit relative to the stars.

## Geostationary orbits
A geostationary satellite orbits about 35,800 km above the equator with a period of one sidereal day, about 23 hours
56 minutes. Because it turns at the same rate as Earth, it stays above the same spot on the ground. That is why
satellite TV dishes can stay pointed at one fixed place in the sky.

## Escape velocity
Escape velocity is the minimum launch speed needed to break free of a body's gravity without further thrust, v = √(2 G
M / r). For Earth it is about 11.2 km/s, roughly 40,000 km/h, and for the Moon only 2.4 km/s. It does not depend on
the mass of the object being launched.

## Kepler's laws of planetary motion
Planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus, and they move faster when closer to the Sun. Kepler's third law
says the square of a planet's orbital period is proportional to the cube of its average distance from the Sun, T² ∝
r³. Mars, 1.52 times farther out than Earth, takes about 1.88 years to orbit.

## Tides
Ocean tides are caused mainly by the Moon's gravity pulling more strongly on the side of Earth nearest to it than on
the far side. This difference stretches the oceans into two bulges, so most coasts get two high tides and two low
tides about every 24 hours 50 minutes. When the Sun and Moon line up, extra-large spring tides occur.
