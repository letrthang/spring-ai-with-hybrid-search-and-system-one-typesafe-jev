# Biotech basics — vaccines

## What a vaccine does
A vaccine trains the immune system to recognise a germ without causing the disease. It shows the body a harmless
version or part of the germ, called an antigen, so the immune system makes antibodies and memory cells. If the real
germ arrives later, the body can fight it off much faster.

## Immune memory
Immune memory is the ability of the immune system to respond faster and more strongly to a germ it has met before.
After a first exposure, long-lived memory B cells and T cells remain in the body for years. A second response can
start within a few days and produce far more antibodies than the first one.

## Live attenuated vaccines
A live attenuated vaccine uses a weakened form of the germ that can still multiply a little but does not cause
serious disease. Examples are the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine and the oral polio vaccine. These vaccines
give strong, long-lasting immunity, but are usually not given to people with a weakened immune system.

## Inactivated vaccines
An inactivated vaccine uses germs that have been killed with heat or chemicals such as formaldehyde, so they cannot
multiply. The injected polio vaccine and many flu vaccines are made this way. Because the germs cannot grow, the
immune response is weaker, and several doses or booster shots are usually needed.

## Subunit and toxoid vaccines
A subunit vaccine contains only one or a few purified pieces of the germ, such as a surface protein. The hepatitis B
vaccine uses a single viral protein made in yeast. A toxoid vaccine, such as tetanus or diphtheria, uses an
inactivated form of the toxin that causes the disease rather than the germ itself.

## mRNA vaccines
An mRNA vaccine gives the body's cells a short-lived messenger RNA recipe for one germ protein, such as the
coronavirus spike protein. The mRNA is wrapped in tiny lipid nanoparticles, and cells use it to make the protein,
which the immune system then learns to recognise. The mRNA is broken down within days and never enters the nucleus.

## Viral vector vaccines
A viral vector vaccine uses a harmless virus, often an adenovirus that cannot multiply, to carry a gene from the
target germ into the body's cells. The cells then make the germ's protein and trigger an immune response. Some Ebola
and COVID-19 vaccines use this approach.

## Adjuvants
An adjuvant is an ingredient added to a vaccine to make the immune response stronger. Aluminium salts have been used
as adjuvants for about 90 years and are the most common. Adjuvants are especially useful in subunit and inactivated
vaccines, which on their own produce a weaker response.

## Herd immunity
Herd immunity is the protection a whole community gets when enough people are immune that a germ cannot spread
easily. It protects people who cannot be vaccinated, such as newborns. The share needed depends on how contagious the
disease is; for measles it is about 95%.

## The cold chain
The cold chain is the system of keeping vaccines at the right temperature from the factory to the patient. Most
vaccines must be stored at 2–8 °C, and some must not freeze. Some mRNA vaccines need much colder storage, from about
−20 °C down to −70 °C, which makes delivery harder.
