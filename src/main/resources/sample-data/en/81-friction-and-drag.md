# Physics basics — friction and drag

## What friction is
Friction is the force that resists two surfaces sliding, or trying to slide, across each other. Friction arises
because even smooth-looking surfaces have tiny bumps that catch and stick together where they touch. Friction always
acts along the surface against the relative motion, and it turns kinetic energy into heat, which is why rubbing your
hands together warms them.

## Static and kinetic friction
Static friction holds an object still and grows to match a push, up to a maximum value, before the object starts to
slide. Once the object moves, kinetic (sliding) friction takes over, and kinetic friction is usually smaller than the
maximum static friction. That is why a heavy box needs a hard shove to get going but is easier to keep moving.

## The coefficient of friction
The maximum friction force is F = μ · N, where N is the normal force pressing the surfaces together and μ is the
coefficient of friction. The coefficient of friction has no units and depends on the pair of materials: about 0.7 for
rubber on dry asphalt, 0.1 for steel on ice and 0.04 for Teflon on steel. A 10 kg box on a floor with μ = 0.3 needs a
push of about 29 N to slide, since N = 10 kg × 9.81 m/s² ≈ 98 N.

## Useful friction
Friction is essential for everyday motion: without friction, shoes would slip, car tyres could not grip the road and
nails would pull out of wood. Brakes work by pressing pads against a disc or drum so that friction turns the vehicle's
kinetic energy into heat. On wet or icy roads the coefficient of friction drops sharply, so stopping distances can
double or more.

## Reducing friction
Friction wastes energy and wears parts down, so engineers reduce friction with lubricants, bearings and smooth
surfaces. Oil or grease keeps metal surfaces apart with a thin film, and ball bearings replace sliding with rolling.
Even so, a typical passenger car loses about a third of its fuel energy to friction in the engine, gearbox, tyres and
brakes.

## Rolling resistance
Rolling resistance is the force that slows a wheel or ball rolling on a surface, caused mainly by the wheel and the
ground flexing as they press together. Rolling resistance is much smaller than sliding friction: a car tyre on asphalt
has a rolling resistance coefficient of about 0.01, compared with about 0.7 for a skidding tyre. Under-inflated tyres
flex more, raise rolling resistance and use more fuel.

## What drag is
Drag is the resistive force that a fluid such as air or water exerts on an object moving through it. Drag in air is
also called air resistance, and it acts opposite to the object's motion relative to the fluid. Unlike sliding
friction, drag grows with speed, so drag barely matters for a person walking but dominates for a car on a motorway or
a racing cyclist.

## The drag equation
At everyday speeds the drag force is F = ½ · ρ · v² · Cd · A, where ρ is the fluid density, v the speed, Cd the drag
coefficient and A the frontal area. Because drag rises with the square of speed, doubling the speed quadruples the
drag, and the power needed to overcome drag rises eightfold. For a car with Cd = 0.3 and A = 2.2 m² at 30 m/s (108
km/h) in air of 1.2 kg/m³, the drag is about 360 N.

## Terminal velocity
Terminal velocity is the constant speed a falling object reaches when air drag grows to equal its weight, so the net
force becomes zero. A skydiver falling belly-down reaches about 55 m/s (around 200 km/h), and opening a parachute
raises the drag so much that the terminal velocity drops to about 5 m/s. A large raindrop reaches only about 9 m/s,
which is why rain does not hurt.

## Streamlining
Streamlining means shaping an object so that a fluid flows smoothly around it, which lowers its drag coefficient Cd. A
flat plate facing the flow has a Cd of about 1.2, a sphere about 0.47, a modern car about 0.25 to 0.3 and a teardrop
shape as little as 0.04. Fish, birds, aircraft and racing cyclists in a tucked position all use streamlining to save
energy.
