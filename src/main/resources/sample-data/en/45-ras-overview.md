# RAS basics — Overview of a recirculating aquaculture system

## What a recirculating aquaculture system is
A recirculating aquaculture system (RAS) is an indoor fish farm that cleans and reuses the same water instead of
letting it flow through once. Water leaves the fish tanks, passes through a chain of treatment steps and returns to
the tanks. A well-run RAS replaces only a small part of its water each day, often 5 to 10 %.

## The main treatment steps in a RAS loop
A typical RAS loop has five treatment steps in a fixed order: solids removal, biofiltration, degassing,
oxygenation and disinfection. A drum filter takes out faeces and uneaten feed, the biofilter converts ammonia to
nitrate, a CO2 stripper removes carbon dioxide, an oxygen cone adds oxygen, and a UV unit kills microbes before the
water returns to the tanks.

## Why farms choose RAS over ponds or sea cages
Farms choose RAS because it gives full control over water quality, temperature and biosecurity in a small footprint.
Fish can grow all year at their ideal temperature, far from storms, algae blooms and wild parasites. A RAS also uses
up to 99 % less water per kilogram of fish than a flow-through farm and keeps waste out of rivers.

## The main drawbacks of RAS
The main drawbacks of RAS are high building costs, high energy use and a strong dependence on working machinery.
Pumps, blowers and oxygen systems run all day and night, so a power cut or pump failure can kill fish within an hour.
Operating a RAS also needs trained staff who understand water chemistry and biology.

## Species commonly grown in RAS
Atlantic salmon smolts, rainbow trout, barramundi, tilapia and whiteleg shrimp are among the species most often grown
in RAS. Salmon smolts are a major use, because land-based tanks let farms grow young salmon to a larger size before
moving them to sea. High-value species are preferred because RAS production costs more per kilogram than ponds.

## Stocking density in a RAS
Stocking density in a RAS is the weight of fish per cubic metre of tank water, measured in kg/m³. Rainbow trout and
salmon smolts are usually kept at 40 to 80 kg/m³, and tilapia can tolerate 60 to 100 kg/m³. Higher densities need
more oxygen, better filtration and closer monitoring, and very high densities can harm fish welfare.

## Hydraulic retention time and tank exchange
Tank exchange rate is how often the full water volume of a fish tank is replaced by treated water, usually once every
30 to 60 minutes in a RAS. A faster exchange carries ammonia and CO2 out of the tank quickly and brings in fresh
oxygen. Too fast a flow, however, makes fish swim too hard and wastes pumping energy.

## The role of the biofilter in a RAS
The biofilter is the living heart of a RAS, because its bacteria remove the ammonia that fish excrete through their
gills. Without a working biofilter, ammonia would reach toxic levels within a day or two. A new biofilter needs four
to eight weeks to build up enough bacteria before the system can be fully stocked.

## Why monitoring matters in a RAS
Continuous monitoring matters in a RAS because water quality can change dangerously within minutes when equipment
fails. Sensors measure dissolved oxygen, pH, temperature, flow and water level around the clock and raise alarms when
values leave safe ranges. A monitoring system lets a small night crew look after many tanks at once.

## Energy use in a RAS
A RAS typically uses 10 to 25 kWh of electricity for every kilogram of fish produced, depending on design and
climate. Water pumps are usually the biggest consumer, followed by heating or cooling, blowers and oxygen production.
Low-head pumps, insulated buildings and heat recovery are the most common ways to reduce energy costs.
