# Physics basics — lasers

## What a laser is
A laser is a device that produces a narrow, intense beam of light of a single colour by stimulated emission. The word
laser stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. The first working laser was built by
Theodore Maiman in 1960 and used a ruby crystal to give red light at 694 nm.

## Stimulated emission
Stimulated emission happens when a passing photon makes an excited atom drop to a lower energy level and release a
second photon. The new photon is an exact copy of the first: it has the same wavelength, travels in the same direction
and is in step, or in phase, with it. Albert Einstein predicted stimulated emission in 1917, and every laser depends
on it to multiply photons.

## Population inversion
A population inversion is the state in which more atoms in a laser medium are in an excited level than in the lower
level below it. Without a population inversion, photons are absorbed faster than they are copied, so no laser beam can
build up. A laser creates the inversion by pumping energy into the medium with a flash lamp, another laser, an
electric discharge or an electric current.

## The optical cavity
The optical cavity of a laser is a pair of mirrors at each end of the laser medium that bounce light back and forth
through it. One mirror reflects almost 100% of the light, while the other, called the output coupler, reflects for
example 99% and lets 1% escape as the beam. Each pass through the medium amplifies the light further, so only light
travelling exactly along the axis grows into a strong beam.

## Monochromatic and coherent light
Laser light is monochromatic, meaning it contains a very narrow range of wavelengths, and coherent, meaning its waves
stay in step with each other. A helium-neon laser, for example, emits red light at 632.8 nm with a spread of much less
than 0.01 nm. Coherence lets laser beams make sharp interference patterns, which is why lasers are used to make
holograms and to measure distances to a fraction of a wavelength.

## Low beam divergence
A laser beam has low divergence, which means it spreads out very little as it travels. A typical laser pointer spreads
by about 1 milliradian, so its spot grows by only about 1 cm for every 10 m travelled. Scientists fire lasers at
mirrors left on the Moon by Apollo astronauts and time the 2.5 s round trip to measure the Earth–Moon distance to
within a few millimetres.

## Common types of laser
Lasers are grouped by their gain medium: gas, solid-state, fibre or semiconductor. A carbon dioxide gas laser emits
infrared light at 10.6 µm and is used for cutting and engraving, while a solid-state Nd:YAG laser emits at 1,064 nm.
Semiconductor diode lasers are the smallest and cheapest, and they power laser pointers, barcode scanners and
fibre-optic links.

## Continuous and pulsed lasers
A continuous-wave laser gives a steady beam, while a pulsed laser packs its energy into very short bursts. A pulse of
1 mJ lasting 10 ns has a peak power of 100 kW, even if the average power of the laser is only 10 W. Ultrafast lasers
make pulses lasting only femtoseconds, 10⁻¹⁵ s, which can cut glass or the cornea of the eye without heating the
material around the cut.

## Everyday uses of lasers
Lasers are used in everyday technology because their beams can be focused to a tiny spot and switched very quickly.
CDs are read at 780 nm, DVDs at 650 nm and Blu-ray discs at 405 nm, and the shorter wavelength makes a smaller spot
that fits more data on a disc. Fibre-optic internet cables carry laser pulses at about 1,550 nm, and lidar sensors in
cars and phones use laser pulses to measure distance.

## Laser safety classes
Laser safety classes rank lasers by how much harm they can do to the eyes and skin. Class 1 lasers, as in CD players,
are safe in normal use, and Class 2 visible lasers below 1 mW are protected by the blink reflex. Class 3R pointers go
up to 5 mW, Class 3B lasers up to 500 mW can injure the eye instantly, and Class 4 lasers above 500 mW can also burn
skin and start fires.
