# RAS basics — Sensors and monitoring

## Which parameters a RAS monitors continuously
A RAS monitors dissolved oxygen, temperature, pH, water level and flow continuously because these can become deadly
within minutes. Many farms also measure ORP, salinity, CO2 and TAN online. Readings are logged every minute or so,
giving operators a full history of every tank.

## Optical dissolved oxygen sensors
Most modern RAS farms use optical DO sensors, which measure oxygen by how it dims the glow of a light-sensitive dye.
They need no membrane changes, no stirring and less calibration than older electrochemical sensors. The sensor cap
wears out and is usually replaced every one to two years.

## pH sensors and their care
A pH sensor measures acidity with a glass electrode and a reference electrode, and it slowly drifts as the glass ages
and the reference becomes fouled. RAS pH probes should be cleaned and calibrated every one to two weeks with two
buffer solutions, usually pH 7 and pH 4 or 10. Most probes last one to two years.

## Online TAN and nitrite measurement
Online TAN sensors measure ammonia with an ion-selective electrode or an automatic wet-chemistry analyser.
Ion-selective probes are cheap but drift and need frequent calibration, while analysers are more accurate but costly.
Many farms instead measure TAN and nitrite by hand with a photometer once a day.

## Flow and level sensors
Flow sensors measure how much water moves through pipes and tanks, while level sensors measure the water height in
tanks, sumps and filters. A sudden drop in flow or level is often the first sign of a pump failure, a blocked pipe or
a leak. These sensors are critical for early alarms.

## Why sensors need calibration
Calibration compares a sensor with a known standard and corrects its reading, because all sensors drift over time.
An uncalibrated sensor may show safe values while fish are in danger, or raise false alarms. Each calibration should
be recorded with the date, the before and after values and the person who did it.

## Checking sensors against a handheld meter
A quick way to check an online sensor is to compare it with a recently calibrated handheld meter in the same water.
If the two differ by more than an agreed amount, such as 0.5 mg/L for DO or 0.1 for pH, the online sensor should be
cleaned and recalibrated. Many farms do this check daily.

## Sensor placement in tanks
Sensors should be placed near the tank outlet, where water quality is worst, and away from oxygen diffusers and inlet
jets. A DO sensor sitting in a stream of bubbles will read too high. Sensors must be fully submerged and protected
from fish bumping into them.

## Data logging and trends
Logging sensor data lets operators spot slow trends, such as a pH drifting down over a week or DO falling a little
lower after each feeding. Trends often reveal problems before they reach alarm levels. Historical data also helps
explain fish losses and plan feeding and maintenance.

## Sensor fouling
Sensor fouling is the build-up of biofilm, algae or scale on a sensor surface, and it is the most common cause of
wrong readings in a RAS. A fouled DO sensor usually reads too low, while a fouled pH sensor responds slowly. Weekly
cleaning with a soft brush and water prevents most fouling.
