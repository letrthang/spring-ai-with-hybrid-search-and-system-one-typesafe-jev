# Physics basics — atoms and radioactivity

## The structure of the atom
An atom has a tiny, dense nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by much lighter electrons. Protons carry a
positive charge, electrons an equal negative charge, and neutrons no charge. The nucleus holds almost all of the mass
but is about 100,000 times smaller than the atom, like a pea in the middle of a stadium.

## Atomic number and mass number
The atomic number is the number of protons in an atom's nucleus, and it decides which element the atom is: carbon
always has 6. The mass number is the number of protons and neutrons together. Carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons,
while sodium, with atomic number 11 and mass number 23, has 12 neutrons.

## Isotopes
Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14 all have
6 protons but 6, 7 and 8 neutrons respectively. Isotopes of an element behave the same chemically, but some, like
carbon-14, have unstable nuclei and are radioactive.

## Alpha radiation
Alpha radiation is a helium nucleus, two protons and two neutrons, thrown out of an unstable nucleus. Alpha particles
are strongly ionising but travel only a few centimetres in air and are stopped by a sheet of paper or the outer layer
of skin. They are most dangerous when an alpha source is swallowed or breathed in.

## Beta radiation
Beta radiation is a fast electron emitted when a neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton. Beta particles travel
about a metre in air, pass through paper, and are stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium. After beta decay the
atomic number goes up by one, so carbon-14 becomes nitrogen-14.

## Gamma radiation
Gamma radiation is a high-energy electromagnetic wave released from a nucleus, often straight after alpha or beta
decay. It has no mass or charge, is weakly ionising and very penetrating, so thick lead or concrete is needed to
absorb most of it. Gamma rays are used to sterilise medical equipment and to treat cancer.

## Half-life
The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the time it takes for half of its nuclei to decay. After two half-lives a
quarter remains, and after three an eighth. Iodine-131 has a half-life of about 8 days, carbon-14 about 5,730 years
and uranium-238 about 4.5 billion years. Heat and pressure cannot change a half-life.

## Radiation dose and the sievert
Radiation dose to the body is measured in sieverts (Sv), usually in millisieverts (mSv). The average person receives
about 2 to 3 mSv a year from natural background radiation, such as radon gas and cosmic rays. A chest X-ray gives
about 0.02 to 0.1 mSv, while a CT scan of the abdomen gives around 10 mSv.

## Nuclear fission
Nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus, such as uranium-235, into two smaller nuclei after it absorbs a
neutron. Each split releases about 200 MeV of energy and two or three more neutrons, which can cause further fissions
in a chain reaction. Nuclear power stations control the chain reaction with control rods that absorb neutrons.

## Nuclear fusion
Nuclear fusion joins light nuclei, such as hydrogen isotopes, into a heavier nucleus, releasing energy because the
product has slightly less mass, following E = m c². Fusion powers the Sun, which turns about 600 million tonnes of
hydrogen into helium every second. It needs temperatures of millions of degrees to overcome the repulsion between
nuclei.
